# Structuring Spanish Learning for English-Speaking Adults: Research & App Curriculum Blueprint

Oct 28, 2026 · @Sam

## Executive summary

**The best-supported structure for English-speaking adults is a week of short daily sessions in which, averaged across the week, roughly a quarter of the time is new material and three-quarters is retrieval, use and recycling of material already met.** New vocabulary should outweigh new grammar by about 60:40 at A1–B1 and about 70:30 by C1. Add no more than one new grammar point per lesson, about two per week. The binding constraint is not how fast adults can take in words: they can learn 30–100 word pairs an hour in the short term ([Elgort 2011](https://www.lextutor.ca/freq/lists_download/elgort_2011.pdf)). It is the review load that each new item creates, about 8–10 reviews a day at steady state for every daily new card ([Anki Manual](https://docs.ankiweb.net/deck-options.html?highlight=fsrs)).

**For your app, design the week, not the day.** Daily lessons should not be homogeneous. Use six lesson types, each about 20 minutes:

- **Introduce:** one new grammar point plus a small word set.
- **Drill/Consolidate:** no new grammar; the latest point is "ground in", interleaved with the previous one.
- **Vocab/Input:** a large word set plus a graded story.
- **Mixed review:** no new items at all.
- **Output/Fluency:** typed conversation and writing with known material.
- **Checkpoint:** a can-do test.

Every type opens with the same constant block of spaced-repetition (SRS) review. New content is budgeted weekly and spread unevenly:

- **A1–A2:** a typical week runs Introduce, Drill, Vocab/Input, Introduce, Drill, Output, Mixed review. That gives 2 new grammar points and about 30–40 new words, with about a fifth to a quarter of the week's time spent on new material.
- **B2–C1:** the rhythm shifts to one or no grammar introductions and more Input and Output days.

These rhythms are design heuristics built from the evidence, not tested schedules. They draw on grammar re-practice gaps of 1–7 days, interleaving across sessions, one new structure per session and the Four Strands balance. A 10-minute "minimum lesson" protects the streak on bad days. An optional listening extension, using the learner's own podcasts and video, carries total study toward Nation's guideline that deliberate study should be no more than about a quarter of learning time ([Nation](https://www.wgtn.ac.nz/lals/resources/paul-nations-publications/paul-nations-publications/publications/foreign-language_1125.pdf)).

**Above the week:** four weekly units, with a Checkpoint day in week 4, make a month. Twelve units plus a consolidation week and a DELE-style benchmark (reading and writing in the app; listening and speaking checked outside it) make a quarter.

**Reaching C1 takes years, not months.** Instituto Cervantes centres programme roughly 480–750 cumulative class hours to complete C1 ([IC San Salvador](https://pro.cervantes.es/documents/d/salvador/folleto-cursos-generales-2026-2-esp); [IC Los Angeles](https://cervantes.org/us/los-angeles/en/learning-teaching/frequently-asked-questions-about-our-spanish-courses)). At 30 minutes a day that is about 32–50 months. At 20 minutes a day it is 4–6 years. The app therefore has to make extra input time attractive, not just the lesson.

**On PCIC alignment, the firmest facts are these:**

- There is no subjunctive at A1–A2.
- The present subjunctive is the only subjunctive tense at B1.
- The preterite and imperfect start at A2.
- The pluperfect starts at B1.
- The Nociones específicas inventory has 20 thematic areas.
- The PCIC sets no hours.

Several popular placements are conflicting or unverified, and these are flagged in the roadmap. Examples are the present perfect at A1, the affirmative imperative at A1, and the future at A2.

## Designing for a text-only app

**Your app is text only: no audio, video or pictures. Every lesson in this document is written for that format. Listening and speaking happen with resources the learner chooses, which the app plans, prompts and tracks but does not host.** This is the format's main trade-off. Two of Nation's four strands (meaning-focused input and output) partly depend on listening and speaking ([Nation](https://www.wgtn.ac.nz/lals/resources/paul-nations-publications/paul-nations-publications/publications/foreign-language_1125.pdf)). The strongest AI-dialogue effects were found for voice plus text, not text alone ([Hou & Min 2025](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/31847710516602398819C5E594038E7B)). Speaking is also the skill that lags most in classroom learners ([CASLS](https://casls.uoregon.edu/wp-content/uploads/pdfs/tenquestions/TBQHoursToReachIH.pdf)). So the app has to make the outside listening and speaking habit easy, regular and visible.

### Where each skill lives

| Skill | In the app (text) | Outside the app (learner's own resources) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reading | Graded dialogues, stories and articles written from the learner's known words | Graded readers; news and fiction from B1 |
| Writing | Typed answers, messages, short texts, and typed chat role-plays with an AI partner | — |
| Listening | A weekly listening goal tied to the week's theme, level-appropriate suggestions, and a minutes log with a one-line check-in ("What was it about?") | Podcasts, YouTube channels, radio, TV. Comprehensible-input channels suit A1–B1; natural podcasts, series and news suit B2–C1 |
| Speaking | Optional "say it out loud" prompts on typed items (the app cannot check them) | Conversation exchanges, tutors, reading the app's dialogues aloud, talking to yourself |
| Pronunciation | Explained in words: stress marked (*teléfono*), comparisons with English sounds ("*j* is like a strong *h*, as in *loch*"), common traps | Listening and imitating |

### Rules for text-only lessons

**Hard rules (always):**

- **No step may depend on sound, image or video.** Where another app would show a picture, set the scene in one line of text ("Lucía's flat: a table, a bed, a sofa by the window.").
- **Every in-app text fits one phone screen or a short scroll.** About 80–150 words at A1, up to 500 at B1, longer only at B2–C1.
- **In-app fluency means fast reading and fast typed recall of known material.** Listening speed is trained outside the app.
- **Track listening and speaking as logged minutes and self-assessed can-dos, never as verified skills.**

**Soft rules (default):**

- **The optional "extension" becomes an outside listening habit:** 10–25 minutes a day, logged, rising in importance from B1. It replaces the in-app audio input that other apps provide, and it is the main route through the B1–B2 plateau.
- **Tie listening to the week's theme:** "This week is food. Find a short video or podcast episode about cooking."
- **Suggest types of resource by level rather than locking learners to one product,** and let them save their own favourites.
- **Prompt reading aloud:** "Read Ana's lines out loud before you reply." This gives some speaking practice even without checking.
- **Spell out sounds only where they trip English speakers:** the tapped and trilled *r*, *ll/y*, *j*, vowels, unaspirated *p/t/k* and stress. Keep it to one tip per lesson.
- **Checkpoints test reading and writing in the app.** For listening and speaking, ask for a can-do self-assessment and recommend an external test (DELE or SIELE) at level boundaries.

## The research base

**The evidence is strong on mechanisms (spacing, retrieval, explicit instruction, interleaving) and weak on exact daily numbers.** No study sets an optimal daily new-word cap, an optimal vocabulary:grammar ratio, or an optimal new:review split. The numbers in this report are therefore of three kinds, and each is labelled.

| Label | Meaning | Examples in this report |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Research-backed** | Experimental or meta-analytic finding, usually not Spanish-specific | Spacing beats massing; retrieval beats re-study; explicit instruction is effective; blocked-then-interleaved grammar practice works |
| **Expert or practitioner guidance** | Published principle or software default, not tested as a ratio | Nation's \~25% cap on deliberate study; Anki's 20 new cards a day and 90% retention default |
| **Design heuristic (derived)** | This report's arithmetic or synthesis from the above | Weekly budget of 30–40 new items and 2 grammar points at A1; lesson types and weekly rhythms; minute-by-minute templates; months per level |

### Spacing and retrieval are the two strongest levers

**Spaced review beats massed review by large margins.** In Cepeda et al. (2008), with 1,354 adults, recall was 64% higher at the optimal gap than with no gap. The optimal gap was about 20–40% of a one-week retention interval and 5–10% of a one-year interval ([Cepeda et al. 2008](https://www.evullab.org/pdf/CepedaVulRohrerWixtedPashler-PS-2008.pdf)). For second-language (L2) vocabulary, how much spacing there is matters more than whether intervals expand ([Nakata, JALT slides](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/optimizing-computerbased-second-language-vocabulary-learning/72858577)). Spaced schedules gave 27–40% higher odds of correct recall than massed practice in a 2025 replication ([Rogers, Nakata & Chiu](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/C833408A4C3BAD939CA39EA734423BB7)).

**Retrieval with feedback, not re-study, drives retention.** Keeping items in repeated testing gave about 80% recall at one week, versus 33–36% when recalled items were dropped from testing ([Purdue on Karpicke & Roediger 2008](https://www.purdue.edu/uns/x/2008a/080214KarpickeTest.html)). Meta-analyses put the testing effect at about g = 0.50–0.67, and it largely disappears without corrective feedback when items are hard ([Greving & Richter 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02412/pdf)).

**In-lesson accuracy is a misleading signal.** Massed practice looks better during training and worse a week later, in both Nakata's studies and Rogers et al. (2025) ([Rogers, Nakata & Chiu](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/C833408A4C3BAD939CA39EA734423BB7)). Learners also prefer the conditions that teach them less. In Kornell (2009), 72% preferred small flashcard decks even though one larger, more spaced deck gave 65% versus 38% next-day recall ([Learning & the Brain summary](https://www.learningandthebrain.com/blog/flashcard-strategies/)).

### Grammar: explicit, then blocked, then interleaved

**Explicit grammar instruction works for adults.** Norris & Ortega (2000), Spada & Tomita (2010) and Goo et al. (2015) all found explicit instruction outperforming implicit ([Norris & Ortega](https://www.deepdyve.com/lp/wiley/does-type-of-instruction-make-a-difference-substantive-findings-from-a-9dzVAhpcHv/); [Spada & Tomita via CASLS](https://caslsintercom.uoregon.edu/content/23177); [Goo et al.](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1075/sibil.48.18goo/html)). The largest synthesis, 54 studies, found an overall instruction effect of g = 1.06 but only a "minor difference" between explicit and implicit ([Kang et al. 2019 via Switchboard](https://www.switchboardta.org/evidence/thirty-five-years-of-isla-on-form-focused-instruction-a-meta-analysis/)). Brief explanation is safe; long explanation is not needed.

**A Spanish-specific study supports the hybrid schedule.** For preterite and imperfect conjugation, interleaving across two weekly sessions beat blocked practice at one week: d = 0.53 on multiple choice and d = 0.79 on short answer. Interleaving within a single session did not help, and it worked best after a blocked introduction ([Pan et al. 2019](https://rickardlab.ucsd.edu/pdf/PTLOR-2019.pdf)). "Hybrid" practice also beat blocked or interleaved practice alone for intermediate learners ([Suzuki & Sunada 2020](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/dynamic-interplay-between-practice-type-and-practice-schedule-in-a-second-language/C01D682D82EDBD00AF29965FCA57BAF3)).

**Skill acquisition theory gives the lesson its order.** The sequence runs from explicit rule, to form-focused exercises, to communicative drills, to open meaning-focused tasks. Practice is skill-specific, receptive processing practice is "especially important for adults," and computer practice often lacks timely feedback ([DeKeyser & Criado 2013](https://digitum.um.es/digitum/bitstream/10201/142072/1/DeKeyser%20and%20Criado_Automatization_Skill%20Acquisition%20and%20Practice%20in%20Second%20Language%20Acquisition_2013.pdf)). Comprehension-based grammar practice wins on immediate receptive tests, while production-based practice wins on delayed productive tests ([Shintani, Li & Ellis via CASLS](https://caslsintercom.uoregon.edu/content/23350)). The app needs both.

### Balance across strands is expert guidance, not experiment

**Nation's Four Strands framework is the most direct guidance on lesson balance.** It calls for roughly equal time on meaning-focused input, meaning-focused output, language-focused learning and fluency. Deliberate study should be "no more than one-quarter" of total time, and deliberate grammar "much less than one quarter." Nation writes this explicitly for self-study ([Nation](https://www.wgtn.ac.nz/lals/resources/paul-nations-publications/paul-nations-publications/publications/foreign-language_1125.pdf)). No experiment tested 25/25/25/25 against other splits.

### App efficacy evidence is mostly company-linked

**Apps reliably build beginner receptive skills; communicative ability lags.** A Duolingo-funded 12-week study (≥15 minutes a day, n = 48 completers, no control group) found gains in all four skills, but time in the app was only weakly linked to outcomes ([Smith, Jiang & Peters 2024](https://rebrand.avantassessment.com/wp-content/uploads/The-effectiveness-of-Duolingo-in-developing-receptive-and-productive-language-knowledge-and-proficiency.pdf)). Independent Michigan State work found that "none could use the language communicatively" in one case study. In a Babbel study with a 10-minutes-a-day target, few learners actually reached even an hour a week ([MSU Today](https://msutoday.msu.edu/news/2020/how-effective-are-language-learning-apps)).

**Goal-oriented dialogue systems show medium effects.** Meta-analyses report d = 0.58 on L2 proficiency and g = 0.61 on speaking, but most systems studied were pre-generative-AI ([Bibauw et al. 2022](https://www.lltjournal.org/item-detail/1035); [Hou & Min 2025](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/31847710516602398819C5E594038E7B)).

## The reference frame: CEFR, PCIC and hours

**The PCIC is the content spine, but it is an inventory, not a syllabus, and it sets no hours.** Hours come from individual Cervantes centres, and those centres disagree by roughly 270 hours on the time to C1.

### What the PCIC is and how to use it

**The PCIC (*Niveles de referencia para el español*, 2006/2007) has a general-objectives section plus 12 inventories in five components.** The components are grammatical (grammar, pronunciation, spelling), pragmatic-discursive (functions, pragmatic tactics, genres), notional (general and specific notions), cultural (referents, sociocultural knowledge, intercultural skills) and learning procedures ([García Santa-Cecilia 2007](https://educacionfpydeportes.gob.es/dam/jcr:2f8b7b05-ca26-4c01-9f71-804d6f2aa6af/2007-esp-12-01garcia-pp-pdf.pdf); [Edelsa](https://www.edelsa.es/catalogo/documentos/Niveles_de_Referencia_explicacion.pdf)). Material is split into A1–A2, B1–B2 and C1–C2 blocks, with individual items tagged by level inside each block ([Chacón García 2016](https://www.redalyc.org/journal/921/92153510004/92153510004.pdf)).

**It is a first level of curricular specification that institutions must adapt.** Its level placements rest on expert judgement rather than corpus data ([Chacón García 2016](https://www.redalyc.org/journal/921/92153510004/92153510004.pdf)). Its own introduction gives no hours, total duration or course length ([PCIC introduction, pdfcoffee copy](https://pdfcoffee.com/plan-curricular-del-instituto-cervantes-pdf-4-pdf-free.html)). It also excludes methodology and assessment criteria ([García Santa-Cecilia plenary 2007](https://educacionfpydeportes.gob.es/dam/jcr:1ce828c7-3687-4713-9d32-e0d0ca55fe4c/2007-esp-12-02plenariagarcia-pdf.pdf)). DELE exams draw their linguistic content from it ([DELE A1E guide](https://examenes.cervantes.es/sites/default/files/DELE_A1E_v2020_Guia-de-examen.pdf)).

**Map the inventories to app tracks as follows (design heuristic):**

| App track | PCIC inventory | Role |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Grammar spine | 2 Gramática, 3 Pronunciación, 4 Ortografía | One grammar point per lesson |
| Can-do goals | 5 Funciones, 6 Tácticas pragmáticas, 7 Géneros | Unit goal and the output task |
| Vocabulary themes | 8 Nociones generales, 9 Nociones específicas | Weekly theme and word list |
| Culture | 10–12 Referentes, Saberes, Habilidades interculturales | Story and input content |
| Learner skills | 13 Procedimientos de aprendizaje | Onboarding and strategy tips |

**A verification caveat applies to every placement in this report.** The Centro Virtual Cervantes pages could not be read directly while the research notes were compiled. Placements rest on a third-party copy of the PCIC index, on articles quoting the PCIC, and on university programmes that state they follow it (Zaragoza, UB, UV, Granada). Before launch, check each placement against the CVC Gramática pages ([CVC Gramática A1–A2](https://cvc.cervantes.es/ensenanza/biblioteca_ele/plan_curricular/niveles/02_gramatica_inventario_a1-a2.htm); [B1–B2](https://cvc.cervantes.es/ensenanza/biblioteca_ele/plan_curricular/niveles/02_gramatica_inventario_b1-b2.htm)).

### Hours per level: two Cervantes centres disagree

**Cervantes centres plan about 480–750 cumulative class hours to complete C1.** Los Angeles runs 30-hour courses, with 3, 4, 5, 6 and 7 courses for A1 through C1. That is 90, 120, 150, 180 and 210 hours, or about 750 cumulative ([IC Los Angeles](https://cervantes.org/us/los-angeles/en/learning-teaching/frequently-asked-questions-about-our-spanish-courses)). San Salvador plans 60 hours for A1 + A2.1, then 60, 120, 120 and 120 hours, about 480 to C1 ([IC San Salvador](https://pro.cervantes.es/documents/d/salvador/folleto-cursos-generales-2026-2-esp)).

**The external benchmarks are consistent with the upper end:**

- **FSI:** Spanish is Category I, at 600–750 intensive class hours to S-3/R-3 (≈C1), with about 17 hours a week of self-study on top ([LegalClarity summary of FSI](https://legalclarity.org/state-department-languages-fsi-training-and-proficiency-levels/)). Crosswalks place S-3 at about C1 ([Wikipedia: ILR scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ILR_scale)).
- **Dreaming Spanish (unofficial summary):** about 0–100 input hours for A1, 100–200 for A2, 200–400 for B1, 400–600 for B2 and 600–1,000+ for C1 ([comprehensibleinput guide](https://comprehensibleinput.it.com/blog/comprehensible-input-spanish-guide)).
- **School classrooms:** after 630–720 hours, only about 6% of US high-school learners spoke at Intermediate-Mid or better ([CASLS](https://casls.uoregon.edu/wp-content/uploads/pdfs/tenquestions/TBQHoursToReachIH.pdf)).

**Class hours are not app minutes.** A class hour includes live interaction and is usually paired with homework, so app-only learners should plan toward the Los Angeles figures or beyond (inference). ### Exams as checkpoint templates

**DELE gives a ready-made four-skill checkpoint format.** From A1 to C1 there are four tests: reading, writing, listening and speaking. Total duration runs from about 1 h 50 at A1 to 4 h 20 at C1 ([UCM DELE structure 2020](https://ucm.es/data/cont/media/www/pag-93416/DELEEstructura2020.pdf)). Scores are grouped as reading + writing and listening + speaking, each worth 50 points, with at least 30 points needed in each group to pass ([DELE A1E guide](https://examenes.cervantes.es/sites/default/files/DELE_A1E_v2020_Guia-de-examen.pdf)). SIELE is a single four-skill test scored 0–1,000 that reports a CEFR level for each skill ([UCM SIELE](https://www.ucm.es/ccee/siele)).

**In a text-only app, only the reading-and-writing half of this format can be tested.** Listening and speaking depend on the learner's own practice and are best confirmed by an external exam at level boundaries.

## How much new content per day

**Budget new content by the week, not the day.** At A1–A2, a week should carry about 30–40 new vocabulary items (words or chunks) and 2 new grammar points, tapering at C1 to about 20–30 deliberate items and 0–1 points. Spread these unevenly across lesson types, while holding the daily SRS review block constant. The ceiling is set by review time, not by memory capacity. On average this works out to about 4–6 new items a day at A1, but no single day is average.

### What the evidence says about intake

**Short-term intake is fast.** Learners can learn 30–100 word pairs an hour from bilingual pairs (Nation 1980, cited in [Elgort 2011](https://www.lextutor.ca/freq/lists_download/elgort_2011.pdf)). Elgort's advanced learners recalled 94% of 48 new words after about four hours spread across one week, which works out to about 12 words an hour including review ([Elgort 2011](https://www.lextutor.ca/freq/lists_download/elgort_2011.pdf)).

**The best long-run data point is 15 words a day in 30 minutes or less.** One adult English speaker learning Arabic recalled 283 of 300 words after 20 days at that pace. Without further review, recall fell to 149 at 10 weeks, about 53%, while recognition held at 219 ([Fitzpatrick, Al-Qarni & Meara 2008](https://www.lognostics.co.uk/vlibrary/alqarni2008.pdf)). Anki's default of 20 new cards a day, with 10–20 common in practice, is a software convention, not an optimised value ([Anki Forums](https://forums.ankiweb.net/t/how-many-cards-should-i-be-studying-a-day-is-this-a-personal-preference-or-did-anki-set-the-daily-limit-to-20-cards-for-good-reason/49634)).

### Why review time sets the real cap

**Each daily new card creates about 8–10 reviews a day at steady state.** Learning 20 new cards a day leads to "roughly about 200" reviews a day ([Anki Manual](https://docs.ankiweb.net/deck-options.html?highlight=fsrs)). Above a 90% target retention, workload "increases very quickly" ([Anki Manual](https://docs.ankiweb.net/deck-options.html?highlight=fsrs)).

**Derived budget:** assume 6–10 seconds per single-item review (no published figure exists; this is an assumption). Then each daily new item costs about 0.8–1.7 minutes a day of steady-state review, plus about 30 seconds on its first day for 3–5 within-session retrievals. Working that through:

- A 20-minute lesson with about 4 minutes of single-item reviews can sustain only about 3–5 new items a day.
- Reaching a weekly average of 5–6 items a day in that lesson requires multi-item reviews, where one sentence retrieves 3–5 known words at once, and counting the re-encounters in stories and role-plays.
- An extended session lets the rate rise to about 8–10 items a day.

**This arithmetic matches the curriculum arithmetic.** Getting from zero to C1 needs roughly 3,500–4,500 receptive lemmas from the top 5,000 ([Milton & Alexiou](https://www.enl.auth.gr/gala/14th/Papers/English%20papers/Milton&Alexiou.pdf)). Spread over 2–3 years, that is about 4–6 lemmas a day on average. Both routes point to single digits per day, with more at the start.

### Weekly budgets, distributed unevenly (design heuristic)

**The week is the budget unit because grammar needs consolidation days.** A new structure should be re-practised within 1–3 days at beginner level; short gaps of 1–3.3 days beat 7-day gaps for beginners ([Kasprowicz et al. 2019](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/141710/14/KASPROWICZ_et_al_2019_The_Modern_Language_Journal.pdf)). Interleaving pays off across sessions, not within one ([Pan et al. 2019](https://rickardlab.ucsd.edu/pdf/PTLOR-2019.pdf)). Each grammar introduction therefore "owns" the following day as a Drill/Consolidate day, and the two-point week uses four of seven days.

| Level | New grammar points/week | Grammar Introduce days | New deliberate items/week | Daily average | Peak day (Vocab/Input) | Zero-new-item days/week |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A1 | 2 | 2 | 35–40 | \~5.5 | 14–16 | 2 |
| A2 | 2 | 2 | 30–35 | \~4.5 | 12–14 | 2 |
| B1 | 2 (sometimes 1 + a refinement) | 2 | 28–32 | \~4.3 | 12 | 2 |
| B2 | 1–2 (≥half refinement of known points) | 1 | 25–30 | \~4 | 10–12 | 2–3 |
| C1 | 0–1 (focus on form via feedback) | 0–1 | 20–30 (+ incidental tap-to-save from input) | \~3.5 | 10 | 3 |

**How the budget lands on each day type (A1–A2):**

- Introduce days: 1 grammar point and 4–6 words, light so that working memory goes to the rule.
- Drill/Consolidate days: 0 grammar, 0–4 words.
- Vocab/Input days: 12–16 words.
- Output/Fluency, Mixed review and Checkpoint days: 0 new items.

**The daily SRS block stays constant at about 4–5 minutes, whatever the lesson type.** It also absorbs the uneven intake. Each item's steady-state review cost is spread over weeks, so a 15-word Tuesday does not create a 15-word spike in review load. What matters for the review load is the weekly total.

**Throttle new items automatically.** When the due-review backlog exceeds the day's review budget, cut new items before cutting reviews. Dropping items from retrieval produced the largest losses in Karpicke & Roediger ([Purdue](https://www.purdue.edu/uns/x/2008a/080214KarpickeTest.html)). If the throttle fires, it should first convert the next Vocab/Input day into a Mixed review day. It should not trim words evenly across the week. On a "minimum lesson" day, introduce zero new items.

**Introduce no more than one grammar point per lesson, and never on consecutive days.** Working memory holds about four chunks ([Cowan 2001](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/behavioral-and-brain-sciences/article/magical-number-4-in-shortterm-memory-a-reconsideration-of-mental-storage-capacity/44023F1147D4A1D44BDC0AD226838496)). A rule plus its forms, its exceptions and the rule it contrasts with fills that quickly. No study tests "N grammar points per session" directly. The one-per-lesson cap and the "Introduce, then Drill" pairing are inferred from this, from the blocked-then-interleaved findings above, and from DeKeyser's point that declarative knowledge needs time and practice before it proceduralises ([DeKeyser & Criado 2013](https://digitum.um.es/digitum/bitstream/10201/142072/1/DeKeyser%20and%20Criado_Automatization_Skill%20Acquisition%20and%20Practice%20in%20Second%20Language%20Acquisition_2013.pdf)).

### Encounters per item

**Plan about 8–15 retrievals or meaningful encounters before an item counts as known for recognition, and more for production.** Across incidental-learning studies, the repetitions needed ranged from 2 to 20 ([Uchihara et al. 2019, as reported](https://reading-clone.eprints-hosting.org/108688/8/13621688221140521.pdf)). Five to seven retrievals in a session beat one to three on tests more than two weeks later ([Nakata 2017](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/abs/does-repeated-practice-make-perfect-the-effects-of-withinsession-repeated-retrieval-on-second-language-vocabulary-learning/F14BA8A576CD2563D14CEA46E35D842E)). Ten reading encounters gave 88% recognition but only 29% production in Webb (2007) ([ESLPod summary](https://www.eslpod.com/research/how-words-are-learned/)).

**Design rule (derived):** give each item 3–5 spaced retrievals on day one, with other items in between. Then hand it to the spaced-repetition system (SRS) for roughly 5–8 more retrievals over the following weeks and months. Schedule English-to-Spanish production cards separately from recognition cards.

## The content split: vocabulary vs grammar vs skills, new vs review

**Averaged over a week, about 15–25% of lesson time should present new material and 75–85% should recycle what learners already know.** The split varies sharply by lesson type, from about 50% new on Vocab/Input days to 0% on review, output and checkpoint days. Within the new material, vocabulary (including chunks) should get about 60–65% and grammar about 35–40% at A1–B1, shifting to about 65–70% vocabulary at B2–C1. Across the full day, including any extension, deliberate study should fall from about a third at A1 to about a fifth at C1.

### Vocabulary dominates the deliberate share

**Nation's caps imply that vocabulary should take most of the deliberate time.** Deliberate study is capped at about 25% of total time and grammar at "much less than one quarter," which leaves most of the slot for vocabulary and pronunciation ([Nation](https://www.wgtn.ac.nz/lals/resources/paul-nations-publications/paul-nations-publications/publications/foreign-language_1125.pdf)).

**Spanish frequency data justify front-loading vocabulary.** The top 1,000 lemmas cover 87.8% of spoken text and 76–80% of written text. The top 3,000 cover 94.0% and 88–90%, and each further thousand adds less ([Davies 2005](https://www.mark-davies.org/articles/davies_26.pdf)). Counting high-frequency chunks (*me gustaría*, *tengo que*, *¿me puede…?*, *lo siento*) as vocabulary rather than grammar raises the effective vocabulary share at A1 (heuristic, following [Boone & Eyckmans 2020](https://revistas.uvigo.es/index.php/vial/article/download/1463/1437)).

**Vocabulary targets by level (receptive lemmas, indicative):**

| Level | Cumulative target | Added at this level | Basis |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A1 | \~1,000–1,500 | \~1,000–1,500 | Milton & Alexiou CEFR estimates (English, French, Greek); Duolingo used \~800 words per level at A1–A2 |
| A2 | \~1,500–2,500 | \~750–1,000 | Same |
| B1 | \~2,500–3,250 | \~750 | Same; \~94% spoken coverage at 3,000 Spanish lemmas |
| B2 | \~3,250–3,750 + beyond the top 5,000 | \~500 + incidental | Tests cap at 5,000, so they understate |
| C1 | \~3,750–4,500 + beyond the top 5,000 | \~750 + incidental | Written Spanish needs well above 5,000 for 95% coverage (inference) |

Sources: [Milton & Alexiou](https://www.enl.auth.gr/gala/14th/Papers/English%20papers/Milton&Alexiou.pdf); [Duolingo 2019](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-are-duolingo-courses-evolving); [Davies 2005](https://www.mark-davies.org/articles/davies_26.pdf). No Spanish-specific CEFR vocabulary-size study was found, so treat these figures as targets, not standards.

It follows Nation's strands, Shintani's case for both input and output, and the review-load arithmetic. Each extension minute goes to input or fluency.

| New vocabulary and chunks | 3.5 | 3.5 | 3 | 2.5 | 2 |
| New grammar point or grammar practice | 2 | 2 | 2 | 1.5 | 1 |

| Meaning-focused output (typed chat and writing with feedback) | 3.5 | 3.5 | 4 | 4.5 | 5 |

| **Vocab + grammar block share (includes Drill-day grammar practice)** | **28%** | **28%** | **25%** | **20%** | **15%** |

**The lesson alone overshoots Nation's 25% at beginner levels, and that is deliberate.** Beginners have little comprehensible input yet, so a slightly higher language-focused share at A1 is a common adjustment to Nation's balance ([Nation](https://www.wgtn.ac.nz/lals/resources/paul-nations-publications/paul-nations-publications/publications/foreign-language_1125.pdf)). The extension brings total-time shares into line with a curve running from about 30–35% at A1 to 15–20% at C1. Most apps run 70% or more deliberate drills, which is the opposite of this guidance (inference).

### New vs review by lesson type

**The new-versus-review split is a property of the lesson type, and the week averages it out (design heuristic, A1–B1).** In the table, "new" means first-day presentation and retrieval of new words or a new grammar point. Everything else, including grinding this week's grammar on a Drill day, counts as practice of previous material.

| Lesson type | New content (% of minutes) | Of which vocab : grammar | Practice of previous material (%) | Constant SRS block |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Introduce | \~40% | 40 : 60 | \~60% | 4–5 min |
| Drill/Consolidate | \~5–10% (0–4 words) | 100 : 0 | \~90–95% | 4–5 min |
| Vocab/Input | \~50% | 100 : 0 | \~50% | 4–5 min |
| Mixed review | 0% | — | 100% | 6–8 min (extended) |
| Output/Fluency | 0% | — | 100% | 4–5 min |
| Checkpoint | 0% | — | 100% | 3–4 min |
| **A1–A2 week (2 Introduce, 2 Drill, 1 Vocab/Input, 1 Output, 1 Mixed review)** | **\~21%** | **\~67 : 33** | **\~79%** | — |

**"Review" should mean retrieval, recycling and use, not re-study.** The input, output and fluency blocks are built almost entirely from known material, so they also count as practice of previous content. Nation's fluency strand requires "no unknown vocabulary or unknown grammatical features" ([Nation](https://www.wgtn.ac.nz/lals/resources/paul-nations-publications/paul-nations-publications/publications/foreign-language_1125.pdf)). Graded input should keep unknown words to about 2 per 100 (98% coverage) for extensive reading, and Dreaming Spanish calibrates to 95%+ comprehension ([comprehensibleinput guide](https://comprehensibleinput.it.com/blog/comprehensible-input-spanish-guide)).

**Target 85–90% success on reviews.** Ninety percent is the FSRS default and balances retention against workload ([Anki Manual](https://docs.ankiweb.net/deck-options.html?highlight=fsrs)). Workload against retention is U-shaped, and the optimum differs by learner ([FSRS wiki](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/fsrs4anki/wiki/The-optimal-retention)). Lean reviews toward recall and production, because recall decays much faster than recognition ([Fitzpatrick et al. 2008](https://www.lognostics.co.uk/vlibrary/alqarni2008.pdf)).

**Use an adaptive per-item scheduler.** Duolingo's half-life regression cut recall-prediction error to 0.128, against 0.235 for Leitner, and lifted daily activity by 12% in a 3.3-million-user test ([Settles & Meeder 2016](https://preview.aclanthology.org/fix_video/P16-1174.pdf)). FSRS beats SM-2 on about 99.5% of benchmark collections, per a secondary summary ([Mindomax](https://www.mindomax.com/fsrs-vs-sm2-spaced-repetition-algorithm)). Schedule grammar exercises as SRS items too.

## Anatomy of the daily lesson

**There is no single "daily lesson": there are six lesson types that share one frame.** Every type opens with the same constant SRS block and closes with the same progress card. The middle 15 minutes differ by type. Spread across a week, the types walk each grammar point through DeKeyser's stages: explain, then form-focused practice, then communicative practice, then automatisation ([DeKeyser & Criado 2013](https://digitum.um.es/digitum/bitstream/10201/142072/1/DeKeyser%20and%20Criado_Automatization_Skill%20Acquisition%20and%20Practice%20in%20Second%20Language%20Acquisition_2013.pdf)). They also cover Nation's four strands ([Nation](https://www.wgtn.ac.nz/lals/resources/paul-nations-publications/paul-nations-publications/publications/foreign-language_1125.pdf)). All minute marks are design heuristics for a 20-minute lesson at A1–B1.

### The shared frame

| Time | Block | What happens | Evidence anchor |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0:00–0:30 | Goal card | Lesson type, today's can-do (PCIC function) and theme (Nociones específicas area) | Units organised around communication goals ([Duolingo 101](https://blog.duolingo.com/duolingo-101-how-to-learn-a-language-on-duolingo)) |
| 0:30–4:30 | Constant SRS block | Due vocabulary and grammar items, mostly recall and production direction, sentence-level items that cover several words, immediate corrective feedback | Testing effect; feedback matters when items are hard ([Greving & Richter](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02412/pdf)) |
| 4:30–19:30 | Type-specific core | See the templates below | — |
| 19:30–20:00 | Close | Progress toward the unit can-do; preview tomorrow's lesson type; offer the extension | Habit cueing (heuristic) |

### Lesson type summary

| Type | Purpose | New grammar | New words (A1–A2) | New content share | Main strand |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| (a) Introduce | Present one structure declaratively and start mapping form to meaning | 1 | 4–6 | \~40% | Language-focused |
| (b) Drill/Consolidate | "Grind" the latest point from input to controlled output to freer output, interleaved with the previous point | 0 | 0–4 | \~5–10% | Language-focused → output |
| (c) Vocab/Input | Large word set embedded in a graded reading text | 0 | 12–16 | \~50% | Input |
| (d) Mixed review | Retrieval across the last 2–4 units; clear the backlog | 0 | 0 | 0% | Language-focused (retrieval) |
| (e) Output/Fluency | Typed conversation and writing with known material; speed | 0 | 0 | 0% | Output and fluency |
| (f) Checkpoint | Can-do test with delayed items | 0 | 0 | 0% | Assessment |

### (a) Introduce day

| Time | Block | What happens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0:00–4:30 | Frame + SRS | As above |
| 4:30–7:30 | New words | 4–6 items chosen to carry the new structure, each in a short example sentence, with the article and a stress or sound tip where needed; 3–5 interleaved retrievals each |
| 7:30–9:00 | Explicit rule | An explanation of 60–90 seconds' reading with 2–3 model sentences; English support at A1–A2 |
| 9:00–12:00 | Structured input | 8–10 reading items that can only be solved by noticing the form, e.g. "Who did what to whom?" or "past or present?" |
| 12:00–14:30 | Controlled output | 5–6 items: choose the form, then type the form, then transform a sentence |
| 14:30–18:30 | Short reading | A dialogue that uses the structure 5–8 times, with 3 comprehension checks; optional prompt to read it aloud |
| 18:30–19:30 | Quick use | 2 personalised typed sentences with the structure, with feedback on that feature only |
| 19:30–20:00 | Close | As above |

**Why this shape:** explicit instruction is effective for adults ([Kang et al. 2019](https://www.switchboardta.org/evidence/thirty-five-years-of-isla-on-form-focused-instruction-a-meta-analysis/)). Structured input that pushes learners off default strategies is especially useful for Spanish clitics and mood ([Applied Psycholinguistics](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/applied-psycholinguistics/article/offline-and-online-effects-of-processing-instruction/3C7757B196C1CE529EB6CB6D0CEEEA94); [Collentine 2010](https://faculty.weber.edu/tmathews/SLI/Readings/Collentine%202010.pdf)). Introduction is blocked, which Pan et al. found was the right start ([Pan et al. 2019](https://rickardlab.ucsd.edu/pdf/PTLOR-2019.pdf)). Word count is kept low so that working memory goes to the rule ([Cowan 2001](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/behavioral-and-brain-sciences/article/magical-number-4-in-shortterm-memory-a-reconsideration-of-mental-storage-capacity/44023F1147D4A1D44BDC0AD226838496)).

### (b) Drill/Consolidate day

| Time | Block | What happens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0:00–4:30 | Frame + SRS | As above; the SRS block includes yesterday's grammar items |
| 4:30–5:30 | Rule recall | Learner retrieves the rule before seeing it (one prompt), then a 20-second reminder |
| 5:30–8:30 | Input practice | 8–10 structured-input reading items on the latest point, in new contexts and with new verbs |
| 8:30–12:00 | Controlled output, interleaved | 10–12 typed production items, randomly mixing the latest point (\~60%) with the previous point and 1–2 older contrasts (\~40%) |
| 12:00–15:30 | Communicative drill | A short typed exchange with the AI partner in which the learner must choose the form, e.g. questions about the learner's week |
| 15:30–18:30 | Freer output | A short written answer (3–5 sentences) needing the structure; feedback on that feature only; one retry |
| 18:30–19:30 | Optional new words | 0–4 words, only if the backlog allows |
| 19:30–20:00 | Close | As above |

**Why this shape:** a Drill day falls 1–3 days after the matching Introduce day. That is the gap at which beginners consolidated grammar best (1–3.3 days beat 7 days) ([Kasprowicz et al. 2019](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/141710/14/KASPROWICZ_et_al_2019_The_Modern_Language_Journal.pdf)). The day interleaves across sessions, which improved Spanish conjugation retention by d = 0.53–0.79 ([Pan et al. 2019](https://rickardlab.ucsd.edu/pdf/PTLOR-2019.pdf)). It follows "blocked then interleaved", the hybrid that won in two other studies ([Suzuki & Sunada 2020](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/dynamic-interplay-between-practice-type-and-practice-schedule-in-a-second-language/C01D682D82EDBD00AF29965FCA57BAF3)). Moving from controlled to communicative to freer output is DeKeyser's route from declarative knowledge to procedural skill. Production practice also gives the more durable productive gains ([Shintani, Li & Ellis via CASLS](https://caslsintercom.uoregon.edu/content/23350)). Vary verbs and contexts to avoid the "overly repetitive drills of isolated forms" DeKeyser warns against.

### (c) Vocab/Input day

| Time | Block | What happens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0:00–4:30 | Frame + SRS | As above |
| 4:30–10:30 | New words | 12–16 items from one Nociones específicas area, each in a short example sentence, introduced in one interleaved pool (not mini-blocks), each retrieved 3–5 times; chunks count as items |
| 10:30–16:00 | Graded story | A text that contains every new word at least twice and is otherwise 95–98% known; tap any word for a translation; 4 comprehension checks |
| 16:00–18:30 | Word use | Cued production: English prompt → Spanish chunk; gap-fill from the story |
| 18:30–19:30 | Fluency re-read | Re-read the story against a gentle timer, aiming to finish faster than the first read |
| 19:30–20:00 | Close | As above, plus this week's outside listening suggestion on the same theme |

**Why this shape:** a larger interleaved pool beat small blocks for next-day recall, 65% vs 38% ([Kornell summary](https://www.learningandthebrain.com/blog/flashcard-strategies/)). Five to seven retrievals in a session beat one to three ([Nakata 2017](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/abs/does-repeated-practice-make-perfect-the-effects-of-withinsession-repeated-retrieval-on-second-language-vocabulary-learning/F14BA8A576CD2563D14CEA46E35D842E)). Retrieval-based chunk practice beat highlighting alone ([Boone & Eyckmans 2020](https://revistas.uvigo.es/index.php/vial/article/download/1463/1437)). Concentrating words on one day costs nothing extra in review load, because the SRS spreads their reviews over the following weeks.

### (d) Mixed review day

| Time | Block | What happens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0:00–8:30 | Frame + extended SRS | 8 minutes of due items, with backlog clearing allowed |
| 8:30–13:00 | Grammar remix | 12–15 items randomly mixing every grammar point from the last 2–4 units, plus one "big five" contrast (see the difficulty section) |
| 13:00–17:30 | Re-read | A story from 1–3 weeks ago, with new comprehension questions |
| 17:30–19:30 | Leech rescue | The learner's 5–10 most-failed items, given fresh contexts |
| 19:30–20:00 | Close | As above, plus a prompt to log this week's outside listening |

**Why this shape:** keeping items in retrieval is what drove 80% versus about 35% one-week recall ([Purdue on Karpicke & Roediger](https://www.purdue.edu/uns/x/2008a/080214KarpickeTest.html)). Interleaving across units is where the across-session benefit appears ([Pan et al. 2019](https://rickardlab.ucsd.edu/pdf/PTLOR-2019.pdf)). This is also the type the backlog throttle switches to.

### (e) Output/Fluency day

| Time | Block | What happens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0:00–4:30 | Frame + SRS | As above |
| 4:30–6:00 | Task brief | A goal-oriented scenario that uses the week's can-do, e.g. book a room or explain a problem |
| 6:00–12:00 | AI chat role-play | A typed conversation with an outcome; feedback on one feature (usually this week's grammar point); the learner rewrites that turn. Optional: say each reply aloud before typing it |
| 12:00–15:30 | Writing | A short message or paragraph (A1: 3–4 sentences; B1: 80–120 words) with focused feedback |
| 15:30–19:30 | Fluency | Redo the same chat against a timer, or a timed typed retell; only known language |
| 19:30–20:00 | Close | As above |

**Why this shape:** goal-oriented dialogue systems show medium effects on proficiency (d ≈ 0.58), and the largest effects, including g ≈ 0.61 on speaking, came from voice plus text ([Hou & Min 2025](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/31847710516602398819C5E594038E7B); [Bibauw et al. 2022](https://www.lltjournal.org/item-detail/1035)). A typed chat keeps the goal-oriented part but not the voice part, which is why the app should keep pushing outside speaking practice. Nation's fluency strand requires easy, fully known material with pressure to speed up, and Nation recommends output feedback on one feature at a time ([Nation](https://www.wgtn.ac.nz/lals/resources/paul-nations-publications/paul-nations-publications/publications/foreign-language_1125.pdf)). Speaking lags every other skill in classroom learners ([CASLS](https://casls.uoregon.edu/wp-content/uploads/pdfs/tenquestions/TBQHoursToReachIH.pdf)).

### (f) Checkpoint day

| Time | Block | What happens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0:00–3:30 | Frame + short SRS | As above, shortened |
| 3:30–8:30 | Reading | 2–3 short texts with 8 items, including words from 2–4 weeks ago |
| 8:30–14:30 | Writing | One can-do task, scored with a rubric |
| 14:30–17:30 | Interaction | A short typed chat task with an outcome, scored with a rubric |
| 17:30–19:00 | Self-check | Listening and speaking can-do statements rated by the learner, alongside the month's logged listening minutes |
| 19:00–20:00 | Result | Can-do badge; the weakest items are queued for the next Mixed review day |

**Why this shape:** it follows the DELE's grouping of reading with writing ([DELE A1E guide](https://examenes.cervantes.es/sites/default/files/DELE_A1E_v2020_Guia-de-examen.pdf)). DELE's other group, listening with speaking, cannot be tested in a text-only app, so it is self-assessed here and checked externally at level boundaries. It tests delayed items because in-session accuracy misleads ([Rogers, Nakata & Chiu](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/C833408A4C3BAD939CA39EA734423BB7)).

**Three rules hold across every type.** First, new items must reappear in input and output on later days, not just in the SRS. Second, exercises move from recognition to production within each block, never only recognition. Third, the SRS block is never skipped, even when the core is shortened.

### Minimum lesson: 10 minutes (streak-saver)

**On low-energy days, keep the habit and the reviews and drop the new content.** The minimum lesson is about 5 minutes of the SRS block, a 3-minute re-read of a known story with comprehension checks, and a 2-minute timed typed recall. If the minimum lesson replaces an Introduce day, the week's rhythm shifts by one day; grammar introductions are never compressed. Duolingo found that a streak tied to one lesson a day worked better than one tied to XP, and that the first 7 days are the most fragile ([Lenny's Newsletter](https://lennysnewsletter.com/p/behind-the-product-duolingo-streaks)). A single missed day did not meaningfully slow habit formation in Lally et al., where automaticity took a median of 66 days ([BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/how-form-habit)).

### Extension: +10 to +25 minutes (optional)

**The extension is outside listening with the learner's own resources, plus optional extra reading in the app, with no new deliberate items.** The app suggests what to listen to, tied to the week's theme, and logs the minutes. At A1–A2, suggest slow comprehensible-input videos or beginner podcasts. From B1, natural-speed podcasts, since about 94% spoken coverage at 3,000 lemmas makes spoken input usable before written ([Davies 2005](https://www.mark-davies.org/articles/davies_26.pdf)). At B2–C1, series, radio and news outside the app, and authentic articles inside it, with tap-to-save words feeding the SRS.

### How the anatomy shifts by level

| Feature | A1–A2 | B1 | B2 | C1 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Grammar explanation | 60–90 s, English support, one contrast at a time | 60–90 s, mostly Spanish | ≤60 s, refinement and contrast | Rare; focus on form through feedback on output |
| In-app reading | Short scripted dialogues | Graded stories | Semi-authentic articles and blog posts | Authentic news, opinion and literature |

## Learner experience, rules and example lessons

**The templates above are the skeleton; what the learner actually experiences should feel like a short session with a good tutor, not a checklist of exercises.** Your learners are adults with no linguistics training. Every rule in this section serves that: plain language, a human reason for each step, and blocks that flow into each other. These are design guidelines, not research findings, though several rest on the evidence above (brief explanation, feedback on one feature, retrieval over re-study).

### Voice and flow: rules for every lesson

**Hard rules (always):**

- **No jargon in learner-facing text.** Say "talking about where things are," not "*estar* for location"; "the *was doing* past," not "imperfective aspect." A technical term may appear once, in small print, as a label the learner can look up later ("Teachers call this the imperfect").
- **Open with a human reason.** One sentence on what the learner will be able to say today, with a line they would actually use: "Today: tell someone where your keys are. *¿Dónde están mis llaves? — Están en la mesa.*"
- **Example before rule.** The learner reads and understands the pattern in use before anyone explains it.
- **Never more than about 30 seconds of reading without the learner doing something.**
- **Every correction gives the right version and one plain-language reason.** "Almost. *Es* tells you what something is; for where it is, Spanish uses *está*."
- **The internal block names in this document never appear on screen.** The learner does not see "SRS block," "structured input" or "Drill day." They see "Quick warm-up," "Read and pick," "Your turn."
- **Blocks hand off to each other.** The last item of one block sets up the next ("You've seen it a few times. Now try writing it."), not an abrupt "Exercise 4."

**Soft rules (default, may flex):**

- **Tone:** warm, brief and a little playful; a friendly peer, not a professor. Short sentences. No exclamation-mark inflation.
- **Contrast with English the learner already knows:** "English has one *to be*; Spanish splits it in two."
- **Mistakes are normal and predicted:** "Almost everyone says *soy cansado* at first. Tired is a state, so it's *estoy cansado*."
- **Praise progress, not points:** "Monday you got 3 of 6. Today, 8 of 10."
- **Reviews feel like remembering, not testing:** "Remember how to say *kitchen*?" rather than "Question 1 of 12."
- **Change the rhythm every 2–3 minutes:** alternate quick taps with reading, longer typed answers and chat.
- **Keep a recurring cast and setting across each week** (for example, Lucía and her flatmate in Valencia), so new words and patterns come back naturally in stories, role-plays and reviews.
- **Shrink English as level rises:** roughly 70% of instructions in English at A1, 30% at B1, Spanish only from B2, with an English toggle always available (heuristic).
- **Close with real use and a hook:** the learner says something true about themselves, then sees tomorrow framed as a story beat ("Tomorrow, Lucía loses her phone. You'll help her find it.").

**Say this, not that (examples):**

| Instead of | Say |
| --- | --- |
| "Conjugate the verb in the preterite." | "How would you say *I ate yesterday*?" |
| "Incorrect. Agreement error." | "Close! *Casas* is more than one, so it's *bonitas*." |
| "Lesson 4: Copula *estar* + locative PP" | "Saying where things are" |
| "Review session: 34 cards due" | "Quick warm-up: a few words from this week" |
| "Unit test" | "Check-in: see how far you've come" |
| "Subjunctive triggered by volition verbs" | "When you want someone else to do something" |

### Rules by lesson type across levels

| Lesson type | Hard rules (always) | Soft rules (default) | How it changes A1 → C1 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Introduce | One new pattern only. The learner understands an example before seeing the rule. Explanation of 90 seconds' reading or less, in plain words, with at most one exception. Ends with the learner writing their own sentence. | Introduce through a small situation set in one or two lines of text. Pick 4–6 new words that serve the pattern. Show the contrast with English. Add one sound tip if a new word has a tricky sound. | **A1–A2:** English explanation; the pattern can be met first as a fixed phrase. **B1:** simple Spanish with an English toggle. **B2:** framed as refining what they know ("You already know this past; here's how Spanish makes it hypothetical"). **C1:** rare; replaced by short "notice this" moments drawn from the learner's own writing. |
| Drill/Consolidate | No new grammar. The learner tries to recall the pattern before being reminded. At least a third of items mix in the previous pattern. Ends with freer use (own sentences or a chat turn). | "Same pattern, new places": vary verbs and situations so it never feels repetitive. Show progress since the Introduce day. Dress drills as choices in a story or a short challenge. | **A1–A2:** heavily scaffolded (choose, then type, then write freely). **B1:** choose between 2–3 patterns that compete. **B2:** built around the learner's own most frequent errors ("your top three"). **C1:** rewrite for precision or register. |
| Vocab/Input | New words always appear in sentences and a story, never only as word pairs. The story is at least 95% known words. Each new word appears at least twice. | Group words around one situation. Include 2–3 easy cognates as confidence boosts. Tap any word to see a translation. Continue the week's characters. Close with a listening suggestion on the same theme. | **A1:** dialogues of 80–150 words. **A2:** 150–250 words. **B1:** 300–500 words. **B2:** semi-authentic articles and posts. **C1:** authentic texts with tap-to-save words. |
| Mixed review | No new items. Mixes material from at least three earlier units. Prioritises the learner's weakest items. Framed positively, never as remedial. | Call it a "remix" or "catch-up." Re-read an earlier story so the learner notices it is now easy ("Three weeks ago this was hard"). Prompt them to log the week's listening. | Same shape at every level; from B2 it always includes one of the "big five" contrasts. |
| Output/Fluency | The task has a real-world goal and an outcome. Uses only known language. Feedback on one thing only. The learner gets a second try. | Let the learner choose between two scenarios. The AI partner writes at the learner's level. Praise the communication first, then give the one fix. Show the first and second attempts side by side. Offer a "say it aloud first" option. | **A1:** scripted chat with prompts and a word bank, 3–4 turns. **A2:** guided chat. **B1:** open chat and short written stories. **B2:** argue and persuade over 8–10 typed turns. **C1:** structured written arguments and register shifts. |
| Checkpoint | Tests items from 2–4 weeks ago, through reading, writing and a typed chat, plus a listening and speaking self-check. Results shown as plain can-do statements ("You can book a room and handle a problem"), not just a score. Weak items are scheduled automatically. | Low-stakes framing ("a check-in, not an exam"). Show before-and-after comparisons, including logged listening hours. | About 15 minutes monthly at every level; the quarterly version follows DELE's reading-and-writing format and lengthens with level. |
| Minimum lesson (10 min) | Never introduces anything new. Always includes the warm-up review. | One familiar story re-read, one quick "say it aloud" prompt. | Same at every level. |

**The examples below show what the learner actually reads and does, step by step, in a text-only app.** Quoted text is screen copy written in the learner-facing voice. Times follow the 20-minute templates. All are illustrative heuristics, using one recurring cast: Lucía, her flatmate Ana and their friend Marcos.

#### C1 in brief

**At C1, lessons are built around the learner's own output more than new rules.** A typical Input-and-notice lesson: the learner reads a short opinion column, writes a 150-word reply, and the app points out one pattern in their writing, in plain words: "You used *aunque* + *es* three times. When you're granting a point for the sake of argument, Spanish writers usually say *aunque sea*." New words come from tap-to-save while reading. Tone stays peer-to-peer: at this level, the learner is treated as a capable user being polished, not a student being tested.

## The week, month and quarter

**The week is the unit of design.** Each week has one can-do and one theme, and spreads its new content unevenly across the six lesson types so that every grammar introduction is followed by a consolidation day. Every product that works pairs the daily unit with a visible longer-horizon map ([Duolingo: How to review lessons](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-to-review-lessons-on-duolingo); [Pimsleur](https://wiki.pingponghomelab.duckdns.org/content/wikipedia_en_all_nopic_2026-03/Pimsleur_Language_Programs)). The rhythms below are design heuristics derived from four pieces of evidence, not tested schedules:

- grammar re-practice gaps of 1–7 days
- interleaving across sessions
- one new structure per session
- the Four Strands balance

### The A1–A2 weekly rhythm

| Day | Lesson type | New grammar | New words | Previous-material share of minutes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mon | (a) Introduce point A | A (blocked) | 5–6 | \~60% |
| Tue | (b) Drill A, interleaved with last week's B | 0 | 3–4 | \~92% |
| Wed | (c) Vocab/Input | 0 | 14–16 | \~50% |
| Thu | (a) Introduce point B | B (blocked) | 5–6 | \~60% |
| Fri | (b) Drill B, interleaved with A | 0 | 3–4 | \~92% |
| Sat | (e) Output/Fluency, using A and B in the can-do task | 0 | 0 | 100% |
| Sun | (d) Mixed review; in week 4, (f) Checkpoint | 0 | 0 | 100% |
| **Week** | — | **2** | **\~30–40** | **\~79% (≈21% new)** |

**The rhythm puts each point on a practice ladder.** Point A is introduced Monday, ground in Tuesday, practised again Friday (interleaved with B), used Saturday and remixed Sunday. That is re-practice at +1, +4, +5 and +6 days, and the SRS takes over from there. Beginner studies favoured short early gaps: 1–3.3 days beat 7 days. Intermediate studies favoured longer ones: 7–14 days beat 2–3 days ([Kasprowicz et al. 2019](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/141710/14/KASPROWICZ_et_al_2019_The_Modern_Language_Journal.pdf)). The SRS should therefore lengthen grammar gaps toward 1–2 weeks as the learner's level rises.

**The weekend design follows the data.** Duolingo's daily active users dip 5–10% at weekends, and its Weekend Amulet made learners 4% more likely to return a week later ([Duolingo streak blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-streaks-keep-duolingo-learners-committed-to-their-language-goals/)). Weekends therefore carry no new items, and their lesson types tolerate a swap to the minimum lesson.

### How the rhythm changes by level

| Day | A1–A2 | B1 | B2 | C1 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mon | Introduce A | Introduce A | Introduce or refine A | Vocab/Input (authentic) |
| Tue | Drill A | Drill A | Drill A | Output/Fluency (feedback picks a focus point) |
| Wed | Vocab/Input | Vocab/Input | Vocab/Input | Drill on the focus point (if any) |
| Thu | Introduce B | Introduce B (or refine a "big five" point) | Output/Fluency | Vocab/Input |
| Fri | Drill B + A | Drill B + A | Vocab/Input | Output/Fluency |
| Sat | Output/Fluency | Output/Fluency | Drill: "big five" remediation, interleaved | Vocab/Input |
| Sun | Mixed review | Mixed review | Mixed review | Mixed review |
| **New grammar points/week** | **2** | **2** (≈1.5 truly new) | **1** (≥half refinement) | **0–1** (focus on form) |
| **New deliberate words/week** | **30–40** | **28–32** | **25–30** | **20–30** + tap-to-save |
| **Introduce / Drill / Input / Output / Review days** | 2 / 2 / 1 / 1 / 1 | 2 / 2 / 1 / 1 / 1 | 1 / 2 / 2 / 1 / 1 | 0 / 1 / 3 / 2 / 1 |
| **New share of weekly minutes** | \~21% | \~20% | \~17% | \~15% |

**The shift from B1 onward follows the evidence.** Instruction effects decline with proficiency in some syntheses, and proficiency moderates instruction effects ([Kang et al. 2019](https://www.switchboardta.org/evidence/thirty-five-years-of-isla-on-form-focused-instruction-a-meta-analysis/)). Beyond 3,000 lemmas, each new thousand words adds only 1–3 points of text coverage ([Davies 2005](https://www.mark-davies.org/articles/davies_26.pdf)). The persistent errors English speakers make at B2 need recycling more than new rules ([Minnillo et al. 2024](https://escholarship.org/content/qt8p13v2k7/qt8p13v2k7.pdf)). So input and output days replace introduction days. At B1, a "refine" slot (for example, the experiential perfect or atelic preterite, confirmed at B1 by [Azpiazu Torres 2022](https://www.redalyc.org/journal/921/92169591008/)) counts as an Introduce day but builds on a known form.

**Weekly totals at the standard tier:** about 2.3 hours (20 min/day) or 3.5 hours (30 min/day with the extension). A1–A2 weeks carry 2 grammar points and 30–40 new items. B2–C1 weeks carry 0–1 new points, 20–30 deliberate items and two or more output days.

### The month (four units plus a checkpoint)

**A month is four weekly units, and a Checkpoint day replaces the fourth Sunday's Mixed review.** At A1–A2 standard tier, a month covers about 4 can-dos, about 8 grammar points, about 120–160 new items and about 10 hours (15 hours with the extension).

**The monthly checkpoint should test delayed retrieval, not just today's lesson.** Run it as a 15-minute check: two short reading texts, a short written message and a typed chat task, plus a listening and speaking self-check against can-do statements alongside the month's logged listening minutes. Include items learned 2–4 weeks earlier, because practice-phase accuracy misleads ([Rogers, Nakata & Chiu](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/C833408A4C3BAD939CA39EA734423BB7)). Follow the checkpoint with a "remix" lesson that interleaves the month's grammar. Show progress on a CEFR-labelled map, which Duolingo now does section by section ([Duolingo: How to review lessons](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-to-review-lessons-on-duolingo)).

### The quarter (one sub-level)

**A quarter is 13 weeks: 12 units plus one consolidation-and-benchmark week.** At the standard tier, it covers about 12 can-dos, 20–24 grammar points (A1–B1), 360–480 new items and about 30 hours (about 45 hours with a 10-minute extension). Treat each quarter as a named sub-level (A1.1, A1.2 and so on) so learners see a promotion four times a year.

**The quarterly benchmark should mirror the DELE as far as a text-only app can.** Test reading and writing in the app, scored together like DELE's reading-and-writing group, and require at least 60%, matching DELE's 30 of 50 ([DELE A1E guide](https://examenes.cervantes.es/sites/default/files/DELE_A1E_v2020_Guia-de-examen.pdf)). Listening and speaking are self-assessed against can-do statements and shown next to the quarter's logged listening hours. Below threshold, insert a remediation week built from the learner's weakest SRS items and grammar points rather than repeating units. At level boundaries, recommend an external test (DELE or SIELE), which is the only way to validate listening and speaking.

| Horizon | Units | Can-dos | New grammar points (A1–B1) | New items (A1–A2) | Hours (20 / 30 min/day) | Assessment |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Day | — | Progress toward 1 | 0–1 (by lesson type) | 0–16 (avg \~5) | 0.33 / 0.5 | In-lesson retrieval |
| Week | 1 | 1 | 2 | 30–40 | 2.3 / 3.5 | Saturday typed can-do task; Sunday Mixed review; listening minutes logged |
| Month | 4 | 4 | \~8 | 120–160 | 10 / 15 | Checkpoint day: reading, writing, typed chat, listening and speaking self-check |
| Quarter | 12 + 1 | 12 | 20–24 | 360–480 | 30 / 45 | DELE-style reading-and-writing benchmark (≥60%); listening and speaking self-assessed, external test at level boundaries |

## Level-by-level roadmap A1 to C1

**Sequence grammar to the PCIC where it is confirmed. Where sources conflict, choose the pedagogically useful option and label it as a teaching choice.** Spiral the 20 Nociones específicas areas through every level with denser vocabulary each time. The PCIC tags every area across A1–A2, B1–B2 and C1–C2 ([PCIC index, pdfcoffee copy](https://pdfcoffee.com/plan-curricular-del-instituto-cervantes-pdf-4-pdf-free.html); [Cruz Iturribarri 2018](https://decires.cepe.unam.mx/index.php/decires/article/download/36/30/60)).

**Status key:**

- **CONFIRMED:** the PCIC index or a PCIC-quoting article states it.
- **SUPPORTED:** only PCIC-aligned university programmes state it.
- **CONFLICTING:** sources disagree.
- **UNVERIFIED:** no usable source was found.

Add the future late in A2 for prediction, since *ir a* already covers plans. Themes widen to Salud e higiene, Servicios, Trabajo and Educación.

### B1: entry to the subjunctive (about 8–10 more months)

**B1 introduces mood, but only the present subjunctive.** The present is "el único \[tiempo\] que se estudia en dicho nivel" (the only tense studied at that level) ([Priego-Casanova 2018](https://www.redalyc.org/journal/921/92155180010/92155180010.pdf)). From B1, the outside listening habit should shift toward natural-speed podcasts.

### C1: nuance, register and variation (about 8–14 more months)

**C1 is the least verified part of the PCIC map, so build it around function, genre and input more than new grammar.** The confirmed C-level items are dialectal variation in the present perfect from C1 ([Azpiazu Torres 2022](https://www.redalyc.org/journal/921/92169591008/)) and, at C2, the pretérito anterior and the preterite's present and future uses ([Muñoz 2022](https://revistes.urv.cat/index.php/rile/article/download/3502/3634/10814)).

**Treat the following placements as UNVERIFIED until checked on the CVC C1–C2 page:**

- low-frequency conditional and concessive connectors (*a no ser que*, *por más que*, *de* + infinitive, *como* + subjunctive)
- fine mood alternation
- the future subjunctive, for recognition only, believed to be C2

**Recommendation:** grammar slots drop to about one a week, mostly feedback-driven focus on form in the learner's own output. Most new vocabulary should come from authentic input via tap-to-save, since beyond 3,000 lemmas each thousand words adds only 1–3 points of coverage ([Davies 2005](https://www.mark-davies.org/articles/davies_26.pdf)). Output tasks should mirror DELE C1, which tests reading with use of language, listening, writing and speaking ([UCM DELE structure](https://ucm.es/data/cont/media/www/pag-93416/DELEEstructura2020.pdf)). Add Religión y filosofía and deepen all 20 areas.

## English-speaker difficulty points and sequencing

**English speakers start with a large vocabulary advantage and a predictable set of grammar traps.** The traps sit wherever Spanish marks a distinction that English does not. They develop slowly in fixed stages, so they need spiral recycling at every level, not one-off units.

### The cognate head start

**About 34% of the General Service List and 75% of Academic Word List headwords have Spanish cognates.** Among academic cognates, 77% are more frequent in Spanish than in English: *terminate* ranks 16,697th in English, while *terminar* ranks 219th in Spanish ([Lubliner & Hiebert 2014](https://textproject.org/wp-content/uploads/papers/Lubiner-Hiebert-2014-An-Analysis-of-English-Spanish-cognates.pdf)). More than 90% of Latin-based cognates are true cognates ([Lubliner & Hiebert 2014](https://textproject.org/wp-content/uploads/papers/Lubiner-Hiebert-2014-An-Analysis-of-English-Spanish-cognates.pdf)).

**Exploit this at A1–A2 (heuristic):** teach suffix correspondences such as *-ción*, *-dad*, *-mente* and *-oso* as productive patterns. Prompt learners to "try the formal English word." Teach false friends (*embarazada*, *ropa*, *asistir*, *éxito*, *actual*) as one small, recycled set. No study has measured how much cognate status reduces the encounters needed, so do not assume cognates need fewer reviews without testing it in your own data.

### The persistent difficulty points

| Difficulty | What research shows | Sequencing | App tactic |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| *Ser/estar* | Stages: no copula → *ser* overused → *estar* + -ndo → *estar* + location → *estar* + adjective; not completed within 2 years of university; advanced learners 85% on *ser* adjectives vs 61% on *estar* adjectives ([VanPatten 2010](https://faculty.weber.edu/tmathews/SLI/Readings/VanPatten%202010.pdf)) | A1 *ser* identity, *estar* + location and *estar* + -ndo; A2 *estar* + state adjectives; B1–B2 extension | Spend effort on *estar*; many contextual interpretation items; *ser* "takes care of itself" |
| Preterite/imperfect | English speakers overuse the preterite; imperfect accuracy flat from the 2nd to the 7th university course; tied to *estar, ser, tener* ([Minnillo et al. 2024](https://escholarship.org/content/qt8p13v2k7/qt8p13v2k7.pdf)) | A2 preterite → imperfect via statives → contrast; B1 narrative foreground and background; recycle to C1 | Blocked then interleaved across sessions ([Pan et al.](https://rickardlab.ucsd.edu/pdf/PTLOR-2019.pdf)); narrative retell tasks |
| Subjunctive | Late and often incomplete; 40–50% mood accuracy after 4 months abroad; volition first, irregular forms predict use; processing instruction helps ([Collentine 2010](https://faculty.weber.edu/tmathews/SLI/Readings/Collentine%202010.pdf)) | A2 chunks (*ojalá*, *quiero que* as a phrase, heuristic); B1 volition → emotion → doubt; B2 past subjunctive and hypotheticals | Make sure subordinate clauses are solid first; split main and subordinate clause steps; structured-input items |
| Gender | Learners default to masculine; \~13–15% accuracy on *-e* and deceptive feminine nouns; determiners carry most errors ([Hispanic Studies Review](https://hispanicstudiesreview.cofc.edu/article/91394)) | From day one | Always teach the article with the noun; flag *la mano, el día, el problema*; feedback on determiners |
| Clitics and word order | First Noun Principle misreads *Lo besa María*; comprehension-only processing instruction improved production too ([Applied Psycholinguistics](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/applied-psycholinguistics/article/offline-and-online-effects-of-processing-instruction/3C7757B196C1CE529EB6CB6D0CEEEA94)) | A2 object pronouns; *se lo* later (level UNVERIFIED) | "Who did what to whom?" reading items: pick the English meaning |
| Null subjects | Learners overuse overt *yo/él*; explicit training improved untimed choice but not real-time reading ([Feroce 2021](https://kuscholarworks.ku.edu/entities/publication/38307de6-f7f6-4a3d-b6b9-b58d66e0b860)) | Model null subjects from A1; discourse rules at B1–B2 | Feedback flags unneeded pronouns in output |
| Pronunciation and listening speed | No long-term English-speaking resident was native-like in voice onset time ([SHLL](https://doi.org/10.1515/shll-2020-2034)); Spanish has a very high syllable rate ([Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/fast-talkers)) | Vowels, unaspirated /p t k/ and the tap/trill from A1; natural-speed outside listening from B1 | One written sound tip per lesson, stress marks, spelling contrasts such as pero/perro; listening and imitation outside the app |

**The cross-cutting design rule is "chunks early, system later, recycle forever."** Explicit rules alone do not make these forms productive, and acquisition is gradual and frequency-driven ([VanPatten 2010](https://faculty.weber.edu/tmathews/SLI/Readings/VanPatten%202010.pdf); [Collentine 2010](https://faculty.weber.edu/tmathews/SLI/Readings/Collentine%202010.pdf)). Tag every SRS item and grammar exercise with its difficulty point, and guarantee each of the big five appears in at least one review slot a week from A2 onward (heuristic).

### The B1–B2 plateau

**Expect a plateau and design for it.** Fewer than one in four learners who reach intermediate go on to advanced ([McQuillan via ESLPod](https://www.eslpod.com/research/intermediate-plateau/); the author sells a course). Remaining vocabulary is lower-frequency and needs many encounters, and key grammar plateaus for years. The remedy is volume of comprehensible input plus targeted processing instruction and feedback on persistent structures. The extension tier exists for this purpose and should become more prominent from B1.

## App design implications, metrics and pitfalls

**Optimise for delayed retention and real output, not for completed lessons or XP.** The most credible criticism of apps is that they build recognition knowledge but not communication, and that time in the app correlates weakly with outcomes ([MSU Today](https://msutoday.msu.edu/news/2020/how-effective-are-language-learning-apps); [Smith et al. 2024](https://rebrand.avantassessment.com/wp-content/uploads/The-effectiveness-of-Duolingo-in-developing-receptive-and-productive-language-knowledge-and-proficiency.pdf)).

### Ten build decisions

1. **Ship three tiers** (minimum 10, standard 20, plus 30–45 minutes), with the streak tied to completing any one lesson ([Lenny's Newsletter](https://lennysnewsletter.com/p/behind-the-product-duolingo-streaks)).
2. **Use an FSRS- or HLR-style scheduler** with an 85–90% target retention. Schedule grammar items in it as well as words.
3. **Add a backlog throttle.** New items drop automatically when the reviews due exceed the day's review budget; reviews are never skipped.
4. **Schedule by lesson type, not by a uniform daily lesson.** Use the six types (Introduce, Drill/Consolidate, Vocab/Input, Mixed review, Output/Fluency, Checkpoint) on a level-specific weekly rhythm. Enforce one grammar point per Introduce day, never on consecutive days, and always follow each Introduce day with a Drill day within 1–3 days.
5. **Order every grammar block the same way:** explanation of 90 seconds or less, structured input, controlled output, then open use later in the week.
6. **Build reading texts from known words** at 95–98% coverage, generated from the learner's known-item set, so recycling happens naturally.
7. **Make AI chat goal-oriented.** Role-plays should have a task outcome, typed turns, and feedback on one feature only. Goal-oriented tasks are where the meta-analytic effects concentrate ([Bibauw et al.](https://www.lltjournal.org/item-detail/1035)); the voice element that adds most is outside this format, so pair each chat with an optional "say it aloud" prompt.
8. **Show the map:** CEFR level, sub-level (quarter), unit can-do, hours studied and listening hours logged, as Duolingo now does by section ([Duolingo: How to review lessons](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-to-review-lessons-on-duolingo)).
9. **Protect the streak without deleting reviews.** Freezes and weekend passes are supported by A/B data and habit research ([Duolingo streak blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-streaks-keep-duolingo-learners-committed-to-their-language-goals/); [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/how-form-habit)).
10. **Publish external validation** by pairing quarterly benchmarks with periodic DELE or SIELE results. Duolingo's April 2026 B2 claim came with no published validation method ([Duolingo: advanced content](https://blog.duolingo.com/courses-teach-advanced-content/)), which leaves room to differentiate.
11. **Make outside listening a first-class feature, not an add-on.** Give weekly suggestions tied to the theme and level, a quick minutes log with a one-line "What was it about?", and streak credit for logged listening. In a text-only app, this is the main pathway for listening and speaking.

### Pitfalls to avoid

**Do not let learner preference set pacing.** Learners prefer small, massed blocks that teach less ([Kornell summary](https://www.learningandthebrain.com/blog/flashcard-strategies/)), and massed practice looks better in-session ([Rogers et al.](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/C833408A4C3BAD939CA39EA734423BB7)).

**Do not reward XP farming.** Repeating easy lessons for points is one suggested reason for the weak link between time and outcomes in the Duolingo study ([Smith et al. 2024](https://rebrand.avantassessment.com/wp-content/uploads/The-effectiveness-of-Duolingo-in-developing-receptive-and-productive-language-knowledge-and-proficiency.pdf)). Award points for reviews and production, not for replays.

**Do not over-weight recognition and drills.** Keep deliberate study at or below about a third of a learner's total time at A1 and a fifth at C1. Do not present the lesson alone as a route to C1: at 20 minutes a day, that takes 4–6 years.

**Do not present unverified PCIC tags as official.** Label teaching choices such as an A1 present perfect, an A1 imperative or an A2 future. Do not quote hours as PCIC norms, because the PCIC sets none.

**Streaks cannot fix weak value.** Duolingo's own team says streaks amplify retention but "can't compensate" for a weak product ([Lenny's Newsletter](https://lennysnewsletter.com/p/behind-the-product-duolingo-streaks)).

### What remains unknown

**Four gaps matter for your roadmap:**

- No controlled study compares sustained daily caps such as 5, 10 or 20 new words a day, with dropout as an outcome.
- No study sets an optimal vocabulary:grammar or new:review ratio.
- No published figure gives seconds per review in an app.
- The per-level PCIC tags for *ir a*, the future, the negative imperative, *se lo*, *por/para*, *ser/estar*, *si* + present and all C1 items were not read from the CVC pages.

Run A/B tests on daily caps and the lesson split using delayed-retention metrics, and have a specialist in teaching Spanish as a foreign language check the CVC Gramática pages before finalising the content map.

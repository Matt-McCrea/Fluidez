# The evidence base, checked (4 November 2026)

A research synthesis written for the Fluidez upgrade plan (`docs/plan-2026-11-04/PLAN.md`). It checks the two earlier research documents in this folder against primary sources and meta-analyses, then states the design principles the plan builds on. Where only a secondary summary or an abstract could be read, that is said. Figures taken from the earlier documents without re-checking are marked "(per BLUEPRINT)".

## 1. What the two earlier documents establish

**BLUEPRINT** (`Structuring Spanish Learning for English-Speaking Adults.md`). Its design rules: design the week, not the day (six lesson types: Introduce, Drill, Vocab/Input, Mixed review, Output, Checkpoint); a fixed 4 to 5 minute review block opens every lesson; new material is about a fifth of a week's minutes; vocabulary outweighs grammar about 60:40 at A1 to B1; at most one new grammar point per lesson, never on consecutive days, each followed by a drill 1 to 3 days later; rule under 90 seconds, then structured input, controlled output, freer output; 30 to 40 new items a week at A1 falling to 20 to 30 at C1; a backlog throttle cuts new items before reviews; FSRS or half-life regression at 85 to 90% retention with production cards scheduled separately; graded reading at 95 to 98% known words; a 10-minute minimum lesson; 480 to 750 class hours to C1. Its evidence base is Cepeda 2008, Karpicke and Roediger, Kornell 2009, Pan 2019, Kasprowicz 2019, Nation's four strands, the explicit-instruction meta-analyses, DeKeyser, PCIC-aligned programmes, Davies' frequency data and Lubliner and Hiebert on cognates, and it labels claims by strength.

**SEQ** (`Fluidez Spanish course sequencing.md`). Teach the engine first: irregular high-frequency verbs as chunks from day 1, unpacked by day 26; gender on day 8; developmental order (ser before estar, me/te before lo/la, *se lo* late, relative *que* before the subjunctive); Peninsular perfecto before the indefinido; contrasts taught separately then mixed in review; mastery gated on comprehension; prompt-first feedback; the PCIC as a parts bin restructured into a 537-day path.

**Weak or unsupported claims in them.** "FSRS beats SM-2 on 99.5% of collections" comes from a blog; the benchmark's own tables do not list SM-2 or Leitner, and SM-2 cannot output recall probabilities. Numbers from blogs or companies: Kornell's "65% vs 38%", the Dreaming Spanish hours, McQuillan's plateau statistic, the Duolingo streak data, Lally's 66 days. "8 to 10 reviews per daily new card" is an Anki rule of thumb and "6 to 10 s per review" is an assumption. "One grammar point per lesson" is inferred from working-memory limits, not tested. The Milton and Alexiou vocabulary estimates are for English, French and Greek, not Spanish. SEQ's perfecto-first case rests on Peninsular usage; its chunks-first evidence comes from school-age learners. BLUEPRINT's output-day evidence (Bibauw 2022, Hou and Min 2025) is about AI dialogue systems. Neither document could read the PCIC itself.

## 2. Design principles with the strongest evidence

1. **Retrieve, don't re-read, and give feedback after every attempt.** Rowland 2014 meta-analysis: testing effect g = 0.50; with feedback g = 0.73, without 0.39. Karpicke and Roediger 2008: items kept in testing about 80% recalled at one week, items dropped from testing 33 to 36%, and students could not predict it. App: every item shown at most once passively, then always retrieved; correct form after every attempt; nothing graduates out of retrieval.
2. **Typed recall by default; recognition only as scaffolding.** Rowland: initial tests requiring cued recall g = 0.61, recognition and free recall 0.29 each (partly confounded with feedback). App: reviews are "type the Spanish" or "type the meaning"; multiple choice only on first exposure and in structured-input items.
3. **Space practice with growing gaps and let the algorithm set them.** Cepeda et al. 2008 (about 1,350 participants): the optimal gap shrinks as a share of the retention interval (20 to 40% for one week, 5 to 10% for one year). Kim and Webb's L2 meta-analysis (98 effect sizes, N = 3,411): medium to large spacing effect; expanding and equal spacing equivalent. App: no hand-crafted ladders; a fitted model.
4. **FSRS on-device at about 0.90 retention.** The open benchmark covers about 10k Anki collections and 350M reviews: FSRS-6 log loss 0.346, FSRS-5 0.356, FSRS-4.5 0.363, half-life regression 0.469. Anki's manual sets 90% as default and warns workload rises very quickly above it; workload against retention is U-shaped and varies by learner. Duolingo's half-life regression raised daily engagement 12% in a live test (Settles and Meeder 2016). App: FSRS locally, 0.85 to 0.90 range; schedule sentence and grammar items too; derive the grade from correctness, hint use and latency (an untested adaptation, see section 5).
5. **Gate new items on review load.** Practitioner rule only: 20 new cards a day leads to roughly 200 reviews a day (Anki manual). App: estimate tomorrow's due minutes; if reviews exceed the budget, cut new items; never drop due reviews.
6. **Bilingual word pairs are efficient, but form recall decays fastest.** Webb, Yanagisawa and Uchihara 2020 (22 studies, 100 effect sizes): immediate gains 60% meaning recall, 58.5% form recall, falling to 39.4% and 25.1% delayed. Elgort 2011: deliberate learning built both explicit and implicit knowledge. Direction interacts with proficiency (Webb 2009; Terai et al. 2021). App: L1 glosses are fine; two FSRS cards per lexeme (meaning, form) in short sentences; expect form cards to lapse more.
7. **Size and order vocabulary by frequency; measure each text's coverage.** Nation 2006: 98% coverage of written English needs 8,000 to 9,000 word families. Schmitt, Jiang and Grabe 2011 (661 learners): comprehension rises linearly with coverage, no threshold (about 50% at 90% coverage, 60% at 95%, 70% at 98 to 99%). Hu and Nation's 98% was "not fully replicated" with 104 adults (Kremmel et al. 2023). Spanish coverage of about 88% spoken and 76 to 80% written at 1,000 lemmas (per BLUEPRINT, Davies 2005). App: store every text's token list; compute coverage against the learner's known set; about 95% for glossed intensive reading, 98% or more for free reading, lower at A1 with glosses since the relation is linear.
8. **Balance Nation's four strands daily** (expert consensus, not experiment): meaning-focused input, meaning-focused output, language-focused study, fluency. App: four small blocks every session.
9. **Structured input before production.** VanPatten and Cadierno 1993: processing instruction beat traditional output practice on interpreting Spanish object clitics and matched it on production. Caveat: DeKeyser and Sokalski 1996 found input practice helps comprehension and output practice helps production. App: introduce each point with 6 to 10 items solvable only by reading the form, then typed production.
10. **Brief explicit rules.** Norris and Ortega 2000 (49 samples): explicit beats implicit (secondary source d = 1.13 vs 0.54; critics note outcome measures favoured explicit learning). Spada and Tomita 2010 (41 studies): explicit better for simple and complex features. App: a rule card of 90 seconds or less around 2 or 3 examples, plain-English labels, a contrast with English.
11. **Practice is skill-specific.** DeKeyser's skill acquisition theory: declarative becomes procedural only through practice in the target behaviour, and gains are task-specific. App: typed fluency needs timed typed production; reading speed needs timed reading; the app does not train listening or speaking and says so.
12. **Introduce confusable forms one at a time, then mix across sessions.** Pan et al. 2019 on Spanish preterite and imperfect: interleaving within one session did not help; across two weekly sessions it gave substantially better one-week results; systematic alternation during study plus random order during practice worked best. Nakata and Suzuki 2019 (115 learners): interleaving caused more errors in training but won at one week. Brunmair and Richter 2019 across fields: g about 0.42, strongest for similar categories, caution for words. App: interleave grammar contrasts in review once both members are taught; do not deliberately interleave vocabulary.
13. **Avoid tight semantic sets together.** Nakata and Suzuki 2019 (SSLA): related sets did not lower posttest scores but produced more interference errors; Tinkham and Waring found negative effects. App: group new words by scenario, not category.
14. **In-session accuracy misleads.** Kornell 2009: spacing beat massing for 90% of participants, yet 72% believed massing worked better. Bjork and Bjork on desirable difficulties. An 85% training success rate is optimal in models (Wilson et al. 2019), not shown for language. App: report delayed retention; tell learners once why reviews feel hard.
15. **Self-correction before the answer.** Lyster and Saito 2010 (15 studies, N = 827): larger effects for prompts than recasts. Li 2010: medium, durable feedback effect. Metcalfe 2017: errors followed by corrective feedback aid learning. App: an anticipated-error library per item; a matched error gets a one-line hint and one retry, then the answer and reason; unmatched errors get a diff; the item returns later in the session.
16. **High-frequency chunks, unpacked later.** Boers et al. 2006 (32 students): chunk-noticing learners rated more proficient. Tavakoli and Uchihara 2020 (56 learners): frequent multiword sequences associated with faster speech (correlational). Boers and Lindstromberg 2012: awareness-raising helps, incidental exposure alone does not. App: early frames (*quiero, puedo, tengo que, voy a* + infinitive, *me gustaría*, *¿me puede...?*), tested in new combinations.
17. **Extensive reading volume is the in-app input strand.** Jeon and Day 2015 (51 contrasts): d = 0.57 overall, adults d = 0.70. Nakanishi 2015: d = 0.46; reading rate d about 0.98 but vocabulary only d about 0.18, so deliberate study is still needed. Incidental learning grows with encounters (Uchihara et al. 2019, r = .34). Day and Bamford's principles: easy material, learner choice, read as much as possible. App: a large graded library with free choice, words-read as the progress measure, light checks after free reading.
18. **Cognates are a measurable head start.** Lubliner and Hiebert 2014: about 34% of the General Service List and 75% of the Academic Word List have Spanish cognates; over 90% of Latin-based cognates are true. The popular "30 to 40%" figure has no stated method. App: a week-1 module on *-tion/-ción, -ty/-dad, -ly/-mente, -ous/-oso, -ic/-ico* plus false friends; cognates count toward coverage and still get reviews until data says otherwise.
19. **Recycle the known hard points in spirals** (per BLUEPRINT and SEQ): ser/estar develops in stages with *estar* + adjective last (VanPatten 2010); English speakers overuse the preterite; the subjunctive comes late, driven by volition and irregular forms (Collentine 2010); clitics are misread under the First Noun Principle; gender errors cluster in determiners. App: tag every item with its hard point; weekly contrast slots from A2; gate on comprehension, track production as lagging.
20. **Design for attrition; keep motivation features modest.** Nielson 2011: of 150 motivated workplace volunteers given Rosetta Stone, 32 passed 10 hours. Al-Hoorie 2018: the ideal L2 self correlates r = .61 with intended effort but r = .20 with achievement. Silverman and Barasch 2023: intact streaks raise engagement, broken ones lower it, a repair softens the drop. About 20% of started Duolingo lessons are not completed. App: 15 to 20 minute default, 5-minute floor, streak repair, about 85 to 90% success per session.

Hours: FSI puts Category I languages at 24 to 30 weeks (600 to 750 class hours); DLI listed Spanish at 26 weeks in 2010.

## 3. What BLUEPRINT and SEQ got right and wrong

**Right:** retrieval plus spacing as the core, a modern scheduler at 85 to 90%, scheduling grammar items, throttling new items under backlog; brief rule then structured input then output; blocked-then-interleaved contrasts across sessions; separate production cards; delayed-retention metrics; prompt-before-answer feedback; gating on comprehension; testing new recombinations; honest hours and evidence labels; SEQ's point that reading gates must check clitics, mood and frequency, not just tense.

**Wrong, or in conflict with the constraints:** AI is built into BLUEPRINT's drill, output and checkpoint days (replace with scripted branching dialogues, anticipated-error matching, self-checked writing against models). SEQ's core decisions are Peninsular (perfecto first, vosotros, leísmo); for neutral Latin American Spanish the preterite is the default past (Schwenter and Torres Cacoullos 2008), *ustedes* only, lo/la, and the PCIC's *centro-norte peninsular* norm needs a Latin American pass on every example. The "first-class outside listening" feature conflicts with no listening, and BLUEPRINT gave in-app reading only 4 to 7 minutes a day.

**Left out:** form recall decaying fastest; semantic-set interference; coverage per learner and per text; deterministic answer checking (accent tolerance with a note, optional punctuation, anticipated errors); turning automatic grading into FSRS grades; cognate rules as a module; attrition evidence; the counter-evidence on processing instruction.

## 4. Ideas from the named methods and books, as text-only features

- **Language Transfer** (Eleftheriou): build each sentence by reasoning from English, "don't try to memorise", cognate conversion. App: "Build it" prompts revealed step by step, plus the cognate module; pair with spaced review since reviewers note endings still need memorising.
- **Michel Thomas:** cognates first, modal + infinitive frames. App: front-load *quiero/puedo/tengo que/voy a* + infinitive.
- **Pimsleur:** anticipation and graduated interval recall (5 s, 25 s, 2 min, 10 min...). App: a within-session ladder for each new item (at once, after a few other items, end of session) before FSRS.
- **Fluent Forever** (Wyner): personal connections, grammar from example sentences. App: personal-sentence cards and cloze cards cut from sentences already read.
- **Olly Richards' Short Stories:** bolded target words, glossary, summary and questions per chapter. App: serial graded stories with chapter summaries that double as fluency re-reads.
- **LingQ:** per-word knowledge states and a known-word count. App: states drive coverage; "known" based on retrieval, not paging past.
- **Dreaming Spanish:** hours-based levels (not peer-reviewed). Reading analogue: cumulative words read mapped to library tiers.
- **Benny Lewis:** a personal conversation script. App: an "about me" script that grows each unit and feeds review cards.
- **Madrigal's Magic Key:** cognate-led early confidence.
- **Keenan, Breaking Out of Beginner's Spanish:** sounding less "gringo", tricky cognates, 64 verbs up close, anglicisms. App: a B1+ "sound natural" track.
- **Practice Makes Perfect: Spanish Verb Tenses:** the model for controlled-practice volume with deterministic checking.
- **Butt and Benjamin, A New Reference Grammar (6th ed.):** the authority for usage notes and region tags.

## 5. Open questions, so we do not over-engineer them

How much explicit teaching helps (VanPatten vs the meta-analyses: keep rules short, input heavy). The 95 vs 98% coverage threshold (linear relation, partial replication: use bands plus comprehension checks). Who benefits from interleaving (prior knowledge moderates it). Card direction by proficiency. Semantic clustering (mixed). Chunks first for adults (untested). Automatic grading into FSRS grades (benchmark data are self-rated). Daily caps and ratios (no study sets them; A/B test on delayed retention). Tasks without AI (TBLT effect revised from d = 0.93 to g = 0.61; constrained text tasks are further from what was studied). App efficacy evidence is mostly company-funded. Whether reading-only input transfers to listening is unknown; assume not.

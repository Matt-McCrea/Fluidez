### gr-sonidos-a1 [grammar A1] Los sonidos: cinco vocales y nada más
SEQ: keep day=3
canDo: pronounce any written Spanish word correctly on sight
summary: Spanish spelling is almost perfectly regular: five vowels, each with exactly one sound, always. English has around twenty vowel sounds and spells them chaotically, which is why an English speaker's instinct is the single biggest source of Spanish mispronunciation.
keywords: hola = hello (the h is silent) | hombre = man | hay = there is, there are | jamón = ham | gente = people | me llamo = my name is | español = Spanish | pero = but | perro = dog | rojo = red | gracias = thank you | cinco = five | vivir = to live | bien = well, good
- Five vowels, five sounds, no exceptions: a as in "father", e as in "bed", i as in "machine", o as in "or", u as in "rule". Each is short, crisp and identical every time it appears. They never blur into the "uh" that English unstressed vowels collapse into — banana in English is three different vowel sounds; banana in Spanish is the same a three times.
- The consonants that surprise: h is always silent: hola, hombre, hay.j and g (before e/i) are a throaty h: jamón, gente.ll and y sound alike, roughly English "y": llamo, yo.ñ is the "ny" of "canyon": español.v and b are the SAME sound: vivir begins like bien.r between vowels is a single tap (pero); rr and initial r are rolled (perro, rojo).
- The one that splits the Spanish-speaking world: In most of Spain, c (before e/i) and z are a "th" sound — gracias ends "thias", cinco starts "thinco". In all of Latin America and southern Spain they are a plain "s". Both are entirely correct; the app's voice is peninsular, so you will hear the "th".
- Why this pays off immediately: Because the spelling is regular, you never have to learn how a Spanish word sounds separately from how it is written. Once these rules are in place you can read any word aloud correctly the first time you meet it — which is not true in English and is one of the genuinely easy things about Spanish.

### gr-acentuacion-a1 [grammar A1] El acento: dónde cae la fuerza y cuándo se escribe
SEQ: merge day=3 target=gr-sonidos-a1
canDo: know which syllable to stress, and why the written accent is there
summary: The written accent is not decoration and it is not optional. Spanish has two default stress rules, and the accent appears on exactly the words that break them — so an accent is a signal that the stress is somewhere you would not have guessed.
keywords: el acento = the accent, the stress | hablo = I speak | habló = he/she spoke | difícil = difficult | imposible = impossible | tú = you | tu = your | él = he | el = the | sí = yes | si = if
- The two defaults: A word ending in a vowel, -n or -s is stressed on the second-to-last syllable: ha-bla-mos, na-ran-ja, jo-ven. A word ending in any other consonant is stressed on the last: ha-blar, ciu-dad, es-pa-ñol. Between them these two rules cover the large majority of Spanish words, and those words carry no accent at all.
- The accent marks the exceptions: When a word does not follow its rule, the accent shows where the stress actually falls: ha-bló ends in a vowel but is stressed on the last syllable, so it needs the mark. Á-ra-be, di-fí-cil, lá-piz — all breaking a default, all marked.
- Why it changes meaning: Stress alone distinguishes real words, and three of these you will meet in the first fortnight: hablo (I speak) versus habló (he spoke); termino (I finish) versus termino (term) versus terminó (he finished). Dropping the accent does not make a small spelling slip — it changes the tense.
- The other accent: telling twins apart: A second job, unrelated to stress: the mark separates pairs of one-syllable words that would otherwise be identical. él (he) / el (the); tú (you) / tu (your); sí (yes) / si (if); qué (what, asking) / que (that). Same sound, different word, and the accent is the only thing distinguishing them in writing.

summary: English points twice — this and that. Spanish points three times, and the third one matters: este is near me, ese is near you, aquel is near neither of us. The system tracks the LISTENER, not just distance, which is why choosing by metres alone goes wrong.
pcic: gramatica:A1:128 gramatica:A1:131 gramatica:A1:132 gramatica:A1:133

### gr-genero-sustantivos-a1 [grammar A1] El género de los sustantivos
SEQ: merge day=8 target=gender-articles
canDo: tell a masculine noun from a feminine one, exceptions included
summary: -o is masculine and -a is feminine most of the time. There are enough exceptions — la moto, el día, el mapa — that the ending is a good first guess rather than a rule, and the common ones are worth learning as whole words.
pcic: gramatica:A1:19 gramatica:A1:21 gramatica:A1:22 gramatica:A1:24 gramatica:A1:25
keywords: el libro = the book | la mesa = the table | el árbol = the tree | la catedral = the cathedral | el corazón = the heart | la solución = the solution | la moto = the motorbike | la foto = the photo | la radio = the radio | el día = the day | el mapa = the map | el problema = the problem
- The default pattern: Most nouns follow -o → masculine, -a → feminine: el libro (the book), la mesa (the table). Consonant endings give no reliable clue by themselves — el árbol (tree), la catedral (cathedral), el corazón (heart), la solución (solution) — so those are learned with their article, though -ción/-sión/-dad/-tud are reliably feminine.
- The famous exceptions: La moto, la foto, la radio LOOK masculine but are feminine — they are shortened forms of longer feminine words (motocicleta, fotografía, radiodifusión) and keep the original gender. El día, el mapa, el planeta go the other way: Greek-origin words in -ma that look feminine but are masculine.
- Heteronimia: a completely different word: Some pairs mark gender with an entirely different word rather than a changed ending: el hombre / la mujer, el padre / la madre, el toro / la vaca.
contrasts: el libro / la mesa (the reliable -o/-a default) | la moto, la foto, la radio (look masculine, are feminine — shortened from feminine words) | el día, el mapa, el planeta (look feminine, are masculine — Greek -ma origin) | el hombre / la mujer (heteronimia: a different word entirely, not a changed ending)
pitfalls: La moto/foto/radio is one of the first things learners get wrong the OTHER way, saying *el moto — memorize this trio as exceptions. | Words in -ma of Greek origin (el problema, el tema, el programa, el sistema) are masculine despite ending in -a. | A consonant ending tells you nothing reliable except for the endings -ción/-sión/-dad/-tud, which are always feminine.

### gr-numero-sustantivos-a1 [grammar A1] El plural de los sustantivos
SEQ: move day=18
canDo: form the plural on the article, the noun and the adjective
summary: Spanish pluralizes by ending: a vowel just takes -s, a consonant needs the fuller -es, and a word already stressed on its last syllable needs both -es and a spelling adjustment.
pcic: gramatica:A1:37 gramatica:A1:38 gramatica:A1:39 gramatica:A1:40 gramatica:A1:41
keywords: el libro / los libros = the book / the books | la casa / las casas = the house / the houses | el profesor / los profesores = the teacher / the teachers | el lápiz / los lápices = the pencil / the pencils | el lunes / los lunes = Monday / Mondays | la ciudad / las ciudades = the city / the cities
- Vowel-final: add -s: mesa → mesas, libro → libros, café → cafés. This is the large majority of Spanish nouns.
- Consonant-final: add -es: hotel → hoteles, ciudad → ciudades, reloj → relojes. Adding a bare -s here (*hotels) is the commonest plural slip English speakers make.
- Stressed on the last syllable: also -es: A word ending in a stressed vowel + consonant, or a one-syllable word ending in a consonant, still takes -es and drops its written accent because the stress no longer falls on the last syllable: autobús → autobuses, inglés → ingleses, francés → franceses.
contrasts: la mesa / las mesas (vowel + s) | el hotel / los hoteles (consonant + es, never bare -s) | el autobús / los autobuses (stressed final syllable: +es, accent drops) | el inglés / los ingleses (same pattern with a nationality)
pitfalls: A consonant-final noun always takes the full -es, never a bare -s: hoteles, not *hotels. | Words like autobús and inglés lose their written accent in the plural, because -es adds a syllable and the stress is no longer on the last one. | Lápiz → lápices: a final -z becomes -c before adding -es, a spelling rule, not an exception to the pattern.

### gr-adjetivos-calificativos-a1 [grammar A1] Adjetivos calificativos
SEQ: merge day=19 target=gr-genero-adjetivo-a1
canDo: describe what something is like
summary: A descriptive adjective can sit right after its noun or, with ser, stand on its own as the predicate — and unlike a possessive, the article lets it stand completely alone, meaning "the [adjective] one."
pcic: gramatica:A1:45 gramatica:A1:46 gramatica:A1:47
keywords: guapo = good-looking | inteligente = intelligent | rojo = red | caro = expensive | carísimo = really expensive | muy = very | el chico = the boy | el jersey = the jumper | el restaurante = the restaurant
- Two positions, one meaning: As a complement it follows the noun directly: el chico guapo. As the attribute of ser, it stands after the verb: El chico es guapo. Both describe the same quality.
- The article can carry it alone: Because el/la already marks gender and number, you can drop the noun and keep just the adjective: el guapo means "the handsome one." A possessive cannot do this — *mi guapo is not a way to say "my handsome one"; you must keep the noun: mi hijo guapo.

contrasts: el chico guapo (adjective as noun complement) | El chico es guapo. (adjective as the attribute of ser) | el guapo (article alone can carry the adjective — the noun is understood) | *mi guapo (a possessive cannot drop the noun the way an article can)
pitfalls: An article can stand in for a dropped noun (el guapo); a possessive cannot — keep the noun after a possessive. | Guapísimo and muy guapo say the same thing at different registers; do not combine them (*muy guapísimo). | Adjective and noun must agree in gender and number even across the verb ser: La chica es guapa, not *guapo.

### gr-genero-adjetivo-a1 [grammar A1] El género del adjetivo
SEQ: move day=19
canDo: make an adjective agree with what it describes
summary: Most adjectives change for gender only if they end in -o; a consonant-ending adjective is normally invariable — except a nationality adjective, which always marks feminine even starting from a consonant.
pcic: gramatica:A1:50 gramatica:A1:51 gramatica:A1:52 gramatica:A1:53
keywords: alto / alta = tall | guapo / guapa = good-looking | feliz = happy (same for both) | español / española = Spanish | alemán / alemana = German | francés / francesa = French | el chico / la chica = the boy / the girl | los ojos = the eyes | el pelo = the hair | azul = blue | corto = short
- The -o/-a pattern: Adjectives ending in -o change to -a for feminine: alto → alta, bonito → bonita.

### gr-articulo-definido-a1 [grammar A1] El artículo definido: distribución
SEQ: move day=41

summary: El/la/los/las sits at the very front of its noun phrase, which is exactly why it can never appear next to a possessive or demonstrative — Spanish allows only one "pointer" at the front at a time.
pcic: gramatica:A1:72 gramatica:A1:74 gramatica:A1:75 gramatica:A1:78 gramatica:A1:80 gramatica:A1:81

### gr-cuantificadores-no-universales-a1 [grammar A1] Poco y mucho
SEQ: move day=76

### gr-temporales-b1 [grammar B1] Temporales: cuando + subjuntivo para el futuro

- Nombres escuetos: syntactic limits: A bare noun (no article, no quantifier) cannot normally stand as the PREVERBAL subject: *Niños juegan en el patio is odd; Spanish prefers En el patio juegan niños, with the bare noun AFTER the verb instead.
contrasts: Cogió un libro y miró el índice. (anaphoric — el índice, a part of what was just named) | Viajaremos a Marruecos. El viaje será largo. (associative — el viaje was implied, not literally named yet)
pitfalls: A bare noun (no article/quantifier) resists standing as the subject BEFORE its verb — move the verb first instead: Llegó gente, not *Gente llegó. | The associative article requires only that the referent be inferable from what was said, not literally pre-named — this is subtler than the ordinary "second mention" rule.

SEQ: move day=215

summary: The pluscuamperfecto marks a past event that had already finished BEFORE another past event — cuando llegamos, ya se había ido — Spanish's equivalent of English's "had done," and it never stands alone without that second past reference point.
pcic: gramatica:B1:432 gramatica:B1:433 gramatica:B1:434 gramatica:B1:435

### gr-relativo-que-a1 [grammar A1] El relativo que
SEQ: keep day=96

pcic: gramatica:A1:261 gramatica:A1:262 gramatica:A1:263 gramatica:A1:264 gramatica:A1:265

### gr-adverbios-afirmacion-negacion-a1 [grammar A1] Sí, no, también, tampoco

summary: The Spanish present does two jobs English splits into two tenses: it reports what is true right now (estamos en clase) and what is generally true (vivo en Madrid) — with no "-ing" form to separate them, so context alone tells you which.

### gr-objeto-directo-a1 [grammar A1] La a personal: Veo a María

pcic: gramatica:A1:461 gramatica:A1:462 gramatica:A1:463

### gr-oraciones-actitud-hablante-a1 [grammar A1] Tipos de oración según la actitud del hablante
SEQ: cut day=—
summary: The same fact — Ana llama por teléfono — can be stated, denied or questioned, and Spanish marks the difference almost entirely with punctuation and word order rather than a different verb form, which is why the question marks at both ends of a Spanish question are not decorative.

### gr-oraciones-finales-a1 [grammar A1] Oraciones finales: para + infinitivo
SEQ: keep day=80
canDo: say what something is for with para

### gr-interrogativos-a1 [grammar A1] Los interrogativos: qué, quién, cuánto, dónde, cómo

- Infinitive as subject: An infinitive clause can BE the subject of another verb: Hablar español es útil — "speaking Spanish" is what is useful, not a person.

- Invariable nouns: gender only visible on the article: A few nouns have one ending for both genders, so only el/la shows which: el/la guía, el/la colega, el/la testigo.

pitfalls: Do not invent a feminine *persona/personoa pair to match a male referent — epicenos are simply exceptions to "gender agrees with sex". | El naranjo and la naranja are not interchangeable stylistic variants; asking for un naranjo at a fruit stand asks for a tree, not fruit. | With el/la testigo, el/la guía, the ending gives no clue — only the article and any adjective agreement tell you the referent's gender.

### gr-numero-sustantivos-b1 [grammar B1] Singularia y pluralia tantum, y el plural en -y
SEQ: make optional day=deep: El or la? (A1–B1)
summary: Some nouns simply do not have the other number: sed and salud never pluralize, ganas and ojeras never appear alone in the singular — and neither gap is a rule you can predict, only one you learn noun by noun.
pcic: gramatica:B1:16 gramatica:B1:17 gramatica:B1:18 gramatica:B1:19
- Singularia tantum: no plural: Cardinal directions and a handful of abstract nouns exist only in the singular: Norte, Sur, Este, Oeste, sed, salud. *Las sedes for "thirsts" is not a word.
- Pluralia tantum: no singular: Some nouns exist only in the plural, even naming one single thing or feeling: ganas (as in tengo ganas), facciones (facial features), ojeras (under-eye circles) — there is no *una gana in this sense.
- Plural of nouns ending in -y: Nouns ending in a consonantal -y add -es and the y becomes part of a full syllable: rey → reyes, ley → leyes, buey → bueyes.
contrasts: Tengo mucha sed. (singularia tantum — no plural exists) | Tiene ganas de salir. (pluralia tantum — no singular in this sense) | El rey y la reina llegaron juntos. (singular rey) | Los reyes de España visitaron el museo. (plural reyes — note the -es, not just -s)
pitfalls: Do not force a plural onto sed or salud — *tengo sedes is not Spanish for "thirsts", it is the plural of a completely different word, sede (headquarters). | Ganas meaning desire/inclination has no natural singular — tengo ganas, never *tengo una gana in this sense (a different, rarer use of gana exists but is not this idiom). | Nouns ending in -y take a full extra syllable in the plural (-es), not just -s: leyes, not *leys.

### gr-adverbios-modo-cantidad-b1 [grammar B1] Adverbios de tiempo, cantidad y modo: posición y matiz
SEQ: make optional day=deep: Adverbs, quantifiers and where they sit (B1–B2)
summary: The interesting fact about these adverbs is not their meaning but their BEHAVIOUR: some can move freely inside the verb phrase, some are pinned to one position, and poco vs un poco is a genuine choice between a complaint and a neutral fact, not two ways of saying the same thing.
pcic: gramatica:B1:304 gramatica:B1:305 gramatica:B1:306 gramatica:B1:307 gramatica:B1:308 gramatica:B1:309 gramatica:B1:310 gramatica:B1:311 gramatica:B1:312 gramatica:B1:313 gramatica:B1:314 gramatica:B1:315 gramatica:B1:316
- Deictic vs anaphoric time: Dentro de dos días is deictic — measured from NOW, the moment of speaking. La semana anterior, un día antes, dos días después are anaphoric — measured from a reference point already set in the story, not from today. A narrative in the past uses the anaphoric set; a plan for the future uses the deictic one.
- Poco vs un poco: a real semantic choice: He estudiado poco is a mild complaint — not enough. He estudiado un poco is neutral — some, a bit. Same activity, opposite framing.
- Position rules for manner adverbs: Plain -mente adverbs of action move fairly freely (fácilmente lo hizo / lo hizo fácilmente). Subjective ones like estupendamente sit after the verb or at the end. Resultative ones like totalmente are PINNED after the verb — *Totalmente lo arruinó is wrong.
contrasts: Dentro de dos días tengo un examen. (deictic — measured from today) | Llegó el lunes; dos días después, se marchó. (anaphoric — measured from a point already set in the story) | He estudiado poco para el examen. (implies "not enough" — a mild complaint) | He estudiado un poco para el examen. (neutral — just states an amount)
pitfalls: Do not use dentro de dos días inside a story set in the past to mean "two days later" — that meaning needs the anaphoric dos días después; dentro de only counts from the moment of speaking. | Resultative adverbs like totalmente cannot move to the front: Lo arruinó totalmente, never *Totalmente lo arruinó. | Poco and un poco are not free variants — swapping them changes whether the sentence reads as a complaint or a neutral report.

### gr-formas-no-personales-b1 [grammar B1] Usos independientes del infinitivo, gerundio y participio
SEQ: move day=295
summary: Each non-finite form has a use that stands entirely on its own, without a conjugated verb anywhere nearby: the infinitive as a public instruction, the gerund as a one-word answer, the participle describing the state something was found or seen in. Recognizing these keeps a learner from hunting for a missing main verb that was never going to be there.
pcic: gramatica:B1:527 gramatica:B1:528 gramatica:B1:529 gramatica:B1:530 gramatica:B1:531 gramatica:B1:532 gramatica:B1:533 gramatica:B1:534 gramatica:B1:535 gramatica:B1:536 gramatica:B1:537 gramatica:B1:538 gramatica:B1:539 gramatica:B1:540 gramatica:B1:541 gramatica:B1:542 gramatica:B1:543
- Infinitive: whose action is it, and standing alone: When the same person wants and does something, the infinitive needs no separate subject: Yo quiero ver una película — "yo" controls both verbs. On signs and instructions, the infinitive gives a generalized command to anyone reading: No fumar, Introducir 1 euro en la ranura. It also fills an indirect question: No sé si ir, No sé qué hacer.
- Gerund: a complete answer, and giving directions: A gerund alone answers a question with no verb repeated: —¿Qué haces? —Estudiando. An unusual but real use gives directions with estar: La cafetería está saliendo a la derecha ("you'll come out to the cafeteria on the right"). Postposed, it can give the method: Aprende leyendo ("by reading").
- Participle: describing the state something was found in: A participle can complement a subject or an object with no other verb machinery: Me miró satisfecho (agrees with the subject, "he/she"), Encontré la tienda cerrada (agrees with the object, "la tienda").
contrasts: Yo quiero ver una película. (same subject controls both verbs — no separate "que") | No fumar. (infinitive as a generalized public instruction) | —¿Qué haces? —Estudiando. (gerund alone, a complete answer) | Encontré la tienda cerrada. (participle agrees with the object, "la tienda")
pitfalls: The independent infinitive on signs (No fumar) is impersonal and general — it is not simply a shortened imperative aimed at "tú"; it applies to anyone reading it. | A participle used this way must agree in gender and number with whatever it describes: Me miró satisfecho (masc. subject) vs Me miró satisfecha (fem. subject) vs Encontré la puerta cerrada (fem. object) — check which noun it is attached to. | Enclitic pronouns on an infinitive or gerund attach to the END and can require a written accent: decírselo, explicándomelo — dropping the accent on the gerund form is a common spelling error once two pronouns are attached.

### gr-presente-irregular-a2 [grammar A2] El presente irregular: la bota y el yo raro
SEQ: split day=26 target=gr-presente-irregular-a2 + gr-presente-yo-irregular-a1
canDo: handle the irregular present: the boot and the odd yo
summary: Most "irregular" present-tense verbs are nothing of the kind — they follow two patterns that cover almost all of them. One changes the vowel in four of the six persons and leaves nosotros and vosotros alone; the other changes only the yo form. Learn the two shapes and several hundred verbs stop being exceptions.
pcic: gramatica:A2:331 gramatica:A2:332 gramatica:A2:333 gramatica:A2:334
keywords: querer → quiero = to want → I want | poder → puedo = to be able → I can | dormir → duermo = to sleep → I sleep | jugar → juego = to play → I play | pedir → pido = to ask for → I ask for | tener → tengo = to have → I have | hacer → hago = to do → I do | salir → salgo = to go out → I go out | conocer → conozco = to know → I know | decir → digo = to say → I say
- The boot: The vowel change happens in yo, tú, él and ellos — and never in nosotros or vosotros. Written out in the usual order those four make a boot shape around the two that stay regular. quiero, quieres, quiere, queremos, queréis, quieren.
- Three vowel changes: e → ie: querer → quiero, preferir → prefiero, empezar → empiezo, pensar → pienso.o → ue: dormir → duermo, poder → puedo, volver → vuelvo, costar → cuesta.e → i (only -ir verbs): pedir → pido, servir → sirvo, repetir → repito.And one verb changes u → ue on its own: jugar → juego.
- The odd yo: A second group is regular everywhere except the yo form. Some add a -g-: tengo, pongo, salgo, hago, digo, vengo, oigo. Verbs ending in a vowel + -cer/-cir add -zc-: conozco, parezco, conduzco, traduzco. Everything else in the paradigm is normal: conozco but conoces, conoce, conocemos.
- Both at once: A few do both, so the yo form is doubly odd while the boot still applies to the rest: tener → tengo, tienes, tiene, tenemos, tenéis, tienen. venir and decir behave the same way.
- Spelling that is not irregularity: Some changes only keep the sound: coger → cojo, seguir → sigo, vencer → venzo. The verb is regular; Spanish spelling simply has no other way to write it.
contrasts: quiero, quieres, quiere, queremos, queréis, quieren (e→ie in the boot; nosotros and vosotros untouched) | duermo, duermes, duerme, dormimos, dormís, duermen (o→ue, same shape) | conozco, conoces, conoce, conocemos (only the yo form is odd) | tengo, tienes, tiene, tenemos, tenéis, tienen (both at once: -g- in yo, e→ie in the rest of the boot)
pitfalls: Never carry the change into nosotros or vosotros. *Quieremos and *duermimos are the commonest errors here; it is queremos and dormimos. | The change is in the stem vowel, not the ending. The endings stay exactly the ones you already know: -o, -es, -e, -emos, -éis, -en. | You cannot tell from the infinitive whether a verb changes. Contar becomes cuento but montar stays monto; querer becomes quiero but beber stays bebo. It has to be learnt with the word — which is why the app teaches these verbs from A2 onwards rather than expecting you to guess.
